## Candidate patterns

### P1: Hyperlocal grounding gap in general-purpose AI advisors deployed in extreme-poverty contexts
- **What it is**: General-purpose chatbots (e.g., ChatGPT-via-WhatsApp) handed to very low-income populations answer well on generic questions but degrade sharply on hyperlocal questions — local market prices, which services are actually open, local conditions — because the underlying model has no grounding in that specific place's fast-changing informal-economy data.
- **Evidence**: GiveDirectly's own [EA Forum write-up](https://forum.effectivealtruism.org/posts/pzFa3GGWfYLPNEGpq/what-21-000-whatsapp-messages-reveal-about-ai-utility-in) of its Rwanda pilot (832 recipients, 21,000 inbound WhatsApp messages, Nov 2025–Apr 2026, built with Turn.io per [GiveDirectly's blog](https://www.givedirectly.org/the-robots-work-at-night) / Proto per [proto.cx](https://www.proto.cx/resource/how-givedirectly-is-using-proto-to-bring-ai-to-rural-rwanda---in-kinyarwanda)) states explicitly: "the more rural the setting, the less useful the AI's answers about local markets, services, and conditions." Separately, India's Gramhal had to rebuild its Bolbhav chatbot into "Bolbhav Plus" specifically because farmers complained the market-price data was three days stale (per [Living Library](https://thelivinglib.org/this-chatbot-democratizes-data-to-empower-indias-farmers) / [triplepundit](https://triplepundit.com/story/2024/gramhal-chatbot-india-farmers/796196)).
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: Both reports name the same failure mode (local grounding and data freshness) as something the deployed products did not solve and had to patch around after the fact — not something a reusable architecture handles by default. No general "plug in your region's informal-economy data feed" layer was found; each deployment re-solves it ad hoc.
- **Maturity**: PARTIAL
- **Verdict notes**: `gh search repos` for "local market price chatbot grounding" / "informal economy data feed AI" returned zero results. WebSearch found only adjacent, non-reusable approaches: Overture Maps (Linux Foundation, grounds AI in *formal* places/businesses, explicitly misses unregistered/informal vendors), a farmer-advisory chatbot architecture (arXiv 2606.25647) that grounds via an MCP tool call but is paper-stage and crop-specific, Colombia's "Pipol Bot" (restricted to official DANE statistics, not informal-economy data), and "Collective Narrative Grounding" (arXiv 2601.04201, a proposal, not a built system). No shipped, reusable "plug in your region's informal-economy data" layer exists; the closest things are either formal-data-only or research-stage.

### P3: SMS/feature-phone AI access solves the channel but not the per-message cost economics
- **What it is**: Several startups (teks, ChatMtaani, textgpt) now deliver AI chatbot access over SMS to feature phones for the ~3.45B people without mobile internet — but the underlying complaint is about SMS *pricing*, not channel availability: SMS costs far more per byte than data, so the tool exists but the economics that made it necessary still block adoption.
- **Evidence**: [tech.eu](https://tech.eu/2025/02/14/startup-teks-aims-to-service-the-3-45b-people-lacking-mobile-internet-access-globally/) on teks targeting "3.45B people lacking mobile internet access globally"; [Citizen Digital](https://www.citizen.digital/tech/how-chatmtaani-is-bringing-ai-to-low-budget-phones-through-sms-n329798) on ChatMtaani for "kabambe" feature phones; the Center for Global Development and [NextBillion](https://nextbillion.net/news/analysis-sms-charges-may-be-inhibiting-the-use-of-ai-for-1-billion-people) both frame the actual blocker as "high markups on text messages," naming it as what "will prevent the world's poorest from making use of the technology" even once an SMS-AI channel exists.
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: The tools found are thin SMS wrappers around an existing LLM API — none described compressing/minimizing per-interaction SMS segment count (the actual cost driver named by CGD/NextBillion) as a first-class design constraint. The gap isn't "can AI reach feature phones" (yes) but "does anything optimize the AI's own response protocol to minimize billed SMS segments for ultra-low-income users" (not found).
- **Maturity**: PARTIAL
- **Verdict notes**: `gh search repos` for "SMS chatbot compression segments" / "SMS AI cost optimization" returned zero results. WebSearch found one shipped implementation of the core mechanic — SMS Works' "AI SMS Message Optimiser" (https://thesmsworks.co.uk/blog/AI-SMS-Message-Optimiser/), which demonstrably shrinks outbound messages to fit fewer billed segments (documented example: 162 chars/2 credits → 142 chars/1 credit) — but it's a generic SMS-marketing feature from one vendor, not purpose-built for AI-chatbot response protocols or targeted at the ultra-low-income/feature-phone population CGD and NextBillion describe. No second implementation or any product framing this as a first-class design constraint for underserved-population AI access was found, so the specific gap survives.

### P4: Legal-testimony-grade translation verification layer for asylum/refugee AI translation
- **What it is**: A verification/confidence-flagging layer specifically for AI machine translation used in legal/asylum testimony — catching ambiguous pronoun, tense, or idiom shifts before they're submitted as part of a legal record, rather than general-purpose translation apps that don't flag legal-risk ambiguity at all.
- **Evidence**: [Techdirt](https://www.techdirt.com/2023/04/27/how-refugee-applications-are-being-lost-in-machine-translation/) and [Context.news](https://www.context.news:443/ai/ais-insane-translation-mistakes-endanger-us-asylum-cases) both document a documented case where automated translation flipped "I" to "we" in a Pashto-speaking asylum seeker's statement, creating an apparent inconsistency with her interview that contributed to her case being rejected. [Respond Crisis Translation](https://respondcrisistranslation.org/en/blog/ai-machine-translation-jeopardizing-afghan-asylum-claims) notes government contractors are increasingly substituting AI machine translation for human translators on cost grounds despite this.
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: The documented current mitigation is "use volunteer human translators instead" (Respond Crisis Translation's own model), not an AI tool with built-in ambiguity detection for legal contexts. [Tarjimly](https://usa.inquirer.net/167578/ai-powered-translation-app-tarjimly-bridges-language-gaps-for-refugees) (600,000+ refugees served in 2024) is the closest shipped tool but is a general assistance/communication app, not a legal-testimony-grade verification layer; OpenAI's own usage policy now prohibits its models in migration/asylum decision-making, which further suggests no compliant AI-side fix has displaced the "avoid AI" response.
- **Maturity**: OPEN
- **Verdict notes**: `gh search repos` for "asylum translation verification" / "legal translation ambiguity detection" returned zero results. WebSearch (Techdirt, Lowy Institute, Context.news, Respond Crisis Translation's own 2025 Arabic/Farsi/Pashto/Kurdish LLM test) surfaced only reporting on the failure mode and the current non-AI mitigation (trained human/trauma-informed translators) — no tool, product, or research prototype that flags translation-ambiguity confidence specifically for legal/asylum testimony was found in any channel.

### P5: Diagnosis-to-action gap in AI crop-disease advisory for smallholder farmers
- **What it is**: AI photo-based crop disease diagnosis for smallholder farmers (KilimoAI, Plantix, FarmerChat, Ama KrushAI) is a mature, multi-product category — but the follow-through from "here's the diagnosis" to "here's how you actually get affordable treatment" is explicitly called out as the unsolved part.
- **Evidence**: Cassava-disease researcher Ernest Mwebaze, quoted via [aihub.org](https://aihub.org/2023/03/16/interview-with-ernest-mwebaze-a-machine-learning-based-app-for-diagnosing-plant-diseases/), states plainly that "providing the app alone is not sufficient, the farmers need many other things" beyond the diagnosis itself. [KilimoAI](https://www.thecitizen.co.tz/tanzania/news/national/kilimoai-empowers-farmers-with-crop-disease-detection-5312654) (22,000+ farmers, Swahili-language, Tanzania) and [Plantix](https://www.globalcenter.ai/aorai/use-cases/plantix) (100M+ images analyzed, vendor claim) both center on diagnosis + generic treatment advice, not on closing the loop to affordable local treatment procurement.
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: This is a SATURATED diagnosis layer sitting on top of an OPEN actionability gap — multiple shipped products stop at "here's the recommended treatment" with no integration into local input-supply availability, pricing, or delivery, which is exactly what the domain expert closest to the problem says farmers still lack.
- **Maturity**: SATURATED
- **Verdict notes**: `gh search repos` for "crop disease treatment procurement" / "farmer input supply marketplace AI" returned zero results, but WebSearch overturns the digest's own hypothesis: Plantix's Crop Health API already ships treatment plans integrated with its marketplace, and Plantix has merged with Salesbee specifically to give diagnosed farmers a digital distribution channel to agri-input shops; BigHaat pairs AI diagnosis with an "Agri Input Marketplace" carrying 10,000+ SKUs from 400+ brands delivered to the farmer's door; AgroStar likewise bundles AI crop diagnosis with an integrated product marketplace. That's ≥3 maintained, shipped products closing the diagnosis-to-procurement loop the Mwebaze quote (2023) said was missing — the gap has since been closed by well-resourced agritech platforms, at least in the India market this evidence covers. (Caveat: these are vendor-reported figures with no independent evaluation, but the saturation bar is about existence of competing products, not evaluation quality.)

### P6: No reusable co-design/governance protocol for assistive AI serving communities that distrust outsider-built tech
- **What it is**: A standardized process for building AI accessibility tools *with* (not just *for*) the affected disability community — addressing documented community backlash against AI tools imposed without their input, rather than another point-solution translation/assistance tool.
- **Evidence**: A 2026 CHI paper (surfaced via WebSearch summary, underlying paper not independently re-verified here) reports that "a prominent online deaf community has banned all posts proposing technology ideas intended to 'help' deaf people," and that sign-language AI is seen by parts of that community as a potential threat to "deaf minoritized language and community-based knowledge" rather than a benefit. By contrast, [Rylo](https://rylo.com/sign) markets itself specifically on being "built with the Deaf community" with dictionary entries guided by Deaf linguists — a response to, not a refutation of, the same mistrust.
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: The complaint (an entire community banning the topic) is structural and process-level, not solved by any single vendor's claim of community involvement. No shared, reusable co-design framework or certification that other assistive-AI builders could adopt was found — each vendor asserts community buy-in individually with no common standard to check it against.
- **Maturity**: PARTIAL
- **Verdict notes**: `gh search repos` for "accessible AI co-design framework" / "disability co-design toolkit" returned zero results. WebSearch found several named frameworks addressing the general co-design problem — TACTIC (Ontario Tech University, a four-loop academic co-design process for assistive AI), Google Research's "Natively Adaptive Interfaces" (Feb 2026, a company-internal framework, not an open/adoptable standard), INNklusion (one university's course-based initiative), and an arXiv position paper proposing a three-layer "ability-diverse collaboration" framework — but none is a shared, checkable certification or standard other assistive-AI vendors could adopt; each is either research-stage, single-institution, or one company's own internal practice, matching the digest's claim that no common, reusable standard exists.
