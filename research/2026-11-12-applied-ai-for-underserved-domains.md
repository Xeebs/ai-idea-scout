## Candidate patterns

### P1: On-device diagnostic AI sized for the phones community health workers actually carry, not reference hardware
- **What it is**: A health-diagnostic assistant (symptom triage, visual diagnosis) engineered to run fully offline on the low-RAM Android phones that frontline/community health workers in low-resource settings actually own, rather than assuming a modern smartphone or a network fallback.
- **Evidence**: Crane AI Labs' EaseHealth (adapted MedGemma on Android, field-tested with 268 health workers and 87+ fully offline clinical assessments in Uganda) explicitly flags that "the majority of health workers' mobile phones have insufficient RAM to run these models locally" (WebSearch, `community health worker AI diagnostic tool offline low connectivity open source...`). A separate student-built project, NiDaan (RAG-based offline diagnostic assistant), reported response-time problems and had to fall back to a hosted Groq model to stay usable — i.e. its "no internet required" claim breaks down in practice (same WebSearch).
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: Both the most field-tested product (EaseHealth) and a solo open build (NiDaan) hit the same hardware wall independently and solved it differently/incompletely — one names it as the primary adoption barrier, the other quietly abandons true offline operation. No shared, hardware-budget-aware deployment pattern exists across the projects found.
- **Maturity**: PARTIAL
- **Verdict notes**: GitHub/web search surfaced many independent attempts but no mature shared pattern: Trij (github.com/Mosss-OS/trij, Gemma offline PWA), AI-CareCompanion (github.com/narender-rk10, React Native, ~1.5GB model), ApnaVaidya mlx-swift-gemma-port (iPhone PoC, "next phase" targets cheap Android), AfyaPack, AIndox (Devpost, sub-524MB on-device, prototype), and Crane AI Labs' own EaseHealth (quantized for 8GB Samsung A16 — still above what most CHW phones carry). Each solves the hardware-budget problem narrowly/incompletely for its own stack; none is a maintained, broadly-adopted, hardware-budget-aware deployment layer, and most are hackathon or single-developer builds with no clinical validation.

### P2: Local-language layer for offline diagnostic/advisory assistants, not just English
- **What it is**: The same offline, low-connectivity AI assistants built for health workers and farmers need native support for the local language(s) of the community they serve, not an English-first build with translation bolted on later.
- **Evidence**: IDinsight's free/open-source Diagnostic Agent (built for community health workers in low-resource settings) lists local-language support as "Coming Soon" in its own feature list (WebSearch, `community health worker AI diagnostic tool offline low connectivity open source...`). Separately, the Stanford HAI "Mind the Language Gap" paper and a machinetranslation.com vendor analysis both note that low-resource language pairs (Haitian Creole, Tamazight, Malagasy named specifically) remain the least reliable even in general-purpose translation (WebSearch, `"low-resource language" AI translation open source...`, `site:news.ycombinator.com low resource language OR underserved AI 2026`).
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: The flagship open-source tool in this space still treats local language as a future roadmap item, not a shipped capability — the gap is acknowledged by the builders themselves, not inferred.
- **Maturity**: PARTIAL
- **Verdict notes**: WebSearch found real shipped local-language offline tools but all narrow and single-developer/regional: SwasthyaSathi (offline TFLite classifier + Hindi/Marathi/Bhojpuri voice for ASHA workers), Fasal Mitra (offline, 9 Indian languages, single-developer), HealthVaani (Hindi/English/Hinglish but online-only, ~800 users), Vayal AI (Tamil voice/vision, offline is "future work" not shipped). No independent evaluation of any of these was found, and coverage is India-centric rather than general-purpose — a serious, evaluated, language-agnostic build would be differentiated.

### P3: Shared data/eval commons for the long tail of low-resource languages, beyond the handful of "flagship" ones each vendor picks
- **What it is**: A pooled, reusable corpus + benchmark effort covering many under-served languages at once, instead of each company or paper picking 1-2 "flagship" low-resource languages (Mayan, Egyptian Arabic, etc.) and building a bespoke model.
- **Evidence**: Mundo AI (YC W25) pitches that "roughly three-quarters of the world's non-English speakers are underserved by current AI" and focuses on native-speaker training data; AI-TraLow targets specifically Mayan and Iberian dialects; Fast Company covered Horus as an Egyptian-focused LLM — each a narrow, separately-funded effort (WebSearch, `"low-resource language" AI translation open source...`). A Common Crawl maintainer noted (per an HN-adjacent thread on Meta's Omnilingual MT) that Common Crawl has been running "a low-resource language project for 1.5 years now" — i.e. still an active, unresolved effort (WebSearch, `site:news.ycombinator.com low resource language OR underserved AI 2026`).
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: Multiple well-funded, independent efforts (Mundo AI, AI-TraLow, Horus, Common Crawl's own project) are each solving a slice of the same long-tail-language problem in isolation — a sign no shared commons has absorbed the work yet.
- **Maturity**: SATURATED
- **Verdict notes**: Follow-up search found the pooled commons this pattern calls for already exists and is maintained by well-resourced teams: Meta's Omnilingual MT (arXiv 2603.16309, released March 2026) covers 1,600+ languages with openly released, actively-expanded benchmarks (BOUQuET/Met-BOUQuET, MeDLEy training data for extremely low-resource languages, BLASER 3, OmniTOX) — explicitly built to cover the long tail at once rather than per-vendor flagship languages. Separately, GlotEval (22 benchmarks/7 tasks) and MuBench (61 languages, ACL 2026 Findings) are maintained, open multilingual eval frameworks with the same long-tail focus. The narrow bespoke efforts the digest cites (Mundo AI, AI-TraLow, Horus) still run in parallel, but the "shared commons" gap itself is being actively closed by well-resourced teams, not open.

### P4: Fully open-stack (not cloud-backend-dependent) agricultural advisory assistants for smallholder farmers
- **What it is**: An AI farming-advisory assistant (crop diagnosis, advice over low-bandwidth channels like WhatsApp) that is open end-to-end — model, data, and hosting — rather than an "open" chat layer sitting on a proprietary cloud backend.
- **Evidence**: SciDev.Net reports that expensive, subscription-based AI tools are pricing smallholders out, and that "farmer-led, open-source, and ecological innovations... are sidelined" in favor of big-tech products (WebSearch, `AI for smallholder farmers legal aid rural health "duct tape"...`). Opportunity International's Ulangizi AI (a WhatsApp chatbot usable without broadband) runs on Microsoft Azure, so despite solving the connectivity problem it is "not a fully open stack" (same WebSearch). The AIEP Initiative's arXiv paper (2601.11537) explicitly calls for shared data, common corpora, and evaluation benchmarks as still-needed infrastructure.
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: The most-cited low-bandwidth success story (Ulangizi) is proprietary-cloud-dependent, and the researchers closest to the space are still asking for basic shared infrastructure (corpora, benchmarks) rather than pointing to it existing.
- **Maturity**: PARTIAL
- **Verdict notes**: GitHub/web search confirms real but incomplete attempts: AgroTutor (CGIAR/CIMMYT, open-source, offline capability unconfirmed), a WUR Streamlit/FAISS/ChromaDB local-inference stack built explicitly to avoid cloud cost/latency/privacy concerns, and a hackathon-grade "Farmer's AI-Advisor" that runs fully offline but is a small student project. Counter-evidence of the gap: Rwanda rice-advisory (self-hosted FastGPT backend but still calls Google Gemini API) and VIONEX (Gemini-based "offline-first syncing" app) both still depend on a proprietary cloud model. No fully open end-to-end (model + data + hosting) stack was found.

### P5: Single-seat / sliding-scale AI tooling pricing for micro-nonprofits and solo practitioners serving underserved populations
- **What it is**: AI tool pricing and packaging designed for the realistic team size of a micro-nonprofit (1-3 people) rather than the 5-seat minimums vendors currently require to unlock nonprofit discounts.
- **Evidence**: A nonprofit-AI buyer's guide found via WebSearch reports Claude's nonprofit program gives 75% off but carries a "minimum five seats" requirement, framed explicitly against a 2-person org ("Your org has two people who'd use it"); ChatGPT Team's discounted tier carries the same five-seat minimum (WebSearch, `nonprofit small org AI tool too expensive "duct tape"...`). A separate source frames the resulting workaround as "digital duct tape" — staff pushed back onto patchwork cheap tools lacking automation/integration because enterprise platforms are "entirely inaccessible" for organizations under 200 employees.
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: The gap is structural (vendor seat-minimum policy), not technical, and both cited sources independently converge on the same complaint — current "nonprofit discount" tiers are built for mid-size nonprofits, not the smallest ones most likely to serve the most underserved communities.
- **Maturity**: OPEN
- **Verdict notes**: WebSearch for sliding-scale/income-based single-seat AI pricing for solo nonprofits and practitioners came up empty — every source found (nonprofit AI buyer guides, Clio on legal-AI pricing, nonprofit AI-tools landscape pages) describes only fixed per-seat tiers with minimums (Claude Team ~2-seat plan minimum, ChatGPT Business/Team, Microsoft 365 Copilot ~$25.50/seat/mo) or blanket free/discounted tiers, never a sliding-scale or single-seat-friendly pricing model. No product, repo, or pilot addressing this as a packaging problem was found; it remains pure discussion/complaint-shaped signal.

### P6: Automatic "accessibility-gap closer" — AI that generates an accessible interaction layer for a feature before the vendor ships one
- **What it is**: Middleware/agent that watches a new app feature or UI change and auto-generates the missing accessibility layer (screen-reader semantics, alt-text, captions) during the window between a feature's release and the vendor's own accessibility team catching up.
- **Evidence**: Google Research's own blog frames this explicitly as "the accessibility gap... the delay between the release of a new feature and the creation of an assistive layer for it," and positions its Natively Adaptive Interfaces framework as one response (WebSearch, `assistive technology AI blind deaf accessibility open source gap...`). Separately, NVDA's 2026 roadmap integrating LLMs directly into the open-source screen reader is called "the most consequential technical shift this year" by an independent analysis, but the same analysis notes the most transformative commercial products "carry price points that limit adoption."
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: Google names the gap as a problem its own framework is only beginning to address (not a solved one), and the open-source alternative (NVDA+LLM) is brand-new as of the 2026 roadmap — there is no mature, vendor-agnostic "auto-generate the accessibility layer" tool yet.
- **Maturity**: PARTIAL
- **Verdict notes**: WebSearch found shipped but narrow implementations limited to alt-text/images only: PhotoShelter's AI-generated alt text (launched April 2026), Accessibility Checker's AI remediation (fills gaps only), and Microsoft Office/Edge auto-captioning. None generalizes to the broader "accessibility layer" (ARIA semantics, screen-reader structure, captions) across arbitrary new UI features that the pattern describes, and the American Foundation for the Blind explicitly warns that AI-driven "overlay" fixes are often low-accuracy and conflict with users' own assistive tech. No vendor-agnostic middleware that watches for new features and auto-generates the full missing accessibility layer was found.

### P7: Maintained, free/open-source legal-aid AI copilot ecosystem, not single-release pilots
- **What it is**: An actively maintained, open-source library of AI skills/workflows specifically for civil legal aid and court self-help programs (intake triage, housing-law Q&A, document drafting), as opposed to one-off hackathon tools or proprietary firm products.
- **Evidence**: LawDroid's Legal Aid Plugin for Claude (launched May 2026, ~15 skills, explicitly "fully open-source, requires no separate accounts, does not depend on a SaaS platform") was built, per its own coverage, because "the Claude-pocalypse bypassed legal aid" — i.e. mainstream AI legal tooling wasn't reaching this population until this one plugin (WebSearch, `legal aid AI pro bono open source "has anyone built"...`). Queen's University's OpenJustice and Stanford's Legal Design Lab are both still building "reference architectures" and "sample workflows" rather than a shipped, adopted product; the commercial comparators found (Legora, MLAGPT) are proprietary.
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: The one clearly open-source, maintained attempt is only ~6 months old at time of research; the academic efforts are pre-release; and the search found no evidence of broad adoption across legal aid orgs yet — this reads as a single early entrant in an otherwise proprietary or pre-release field.
- **Maturity**: PARTIAL
- **Verdict notes**: Confirmed via LawNext/LegalTechMonitor/Pulse.bot coverage: LawDroid's Legal Aid Plugin (May 2026, ~15 skills, open-source on GitHub) is real and free, but one analysis flags open maintenance/security sustainability as an open question, and it still requires a Claude subscription. Stanford Legal Design Lab's co-pilots (Gates-funded) are pre-release, planning to publish "sample workflows," not a shipped ecosystem. Free Law Project offers nonprofit infrastructure (CourtListener API access) but not an AI copilot itself. No broad, multi-org adoption evidence found — a single ~6-month-old entrant, not a mature ecosystem.

### P8: Reusable offline-deployment toolkit for humanitarian AI, instead of each NGO/project re-building the offline stack from scratch
- **What it is**: A shared, hardened deployment layer (model packaging, intermittent-connectivity sync, on-device inference) that humanitarian AI projects across different problem domains (health diagnosis, disease-vector identification, crop advisory) could reuse, instead of each one independently solving "how do we run AI with no reliable internet in a crisis zone."
- **Evidence**: SHIFA (built for Sudan, DRC, Somalia, Nigeria, Rwanda) states its own operating assumption as "internet that drops in and out, if it exists at all"; VectorCam (Visilant) is a separately-built open-source, fully-offline smartphone tool for mosquito identification, also built to run with zero internet access (WebSearch, `community health worker AI diagnostic tool offline low connectivity open source...`). Both solve the same "zero/intermittent connectivity, on-device inference" problem independently, for different problem domains, with no shared toolkit between them evident in search results.
- **Why it looks unbuilt/untested**: Two differently-sponsored humanitarian AI projects, in different countries and problem domains, each had to engineer their own offline-deployment stack from scratch — a sign that no general-purpose "humanitarian offline AI deployment" layer exists for them to have reused instead.
- **Maturity**: PARTIAL
- **Verdict notes**: WebSearch surfaced one direct, nascent attempt at exactly this generalized layer — `offline-mcp` on PyPI (Ollama wrapper + open-weights directory + offline/sync deployment guide for East Africa) — but it has low adoption (~68 weekly downloads) and a single maintainer. Wakoma's "OfflineAI" is only a catalog/list of offline tools, not a reusable toolkit itself. SHIFA, VectorCam, and ResQMesh AI Platform each remain independently-engineered, project-specific stacks with no shared layer between them. The gap is real but a serious, better-resourced build on top of the `offline-mcp` idea would be clearly differentiated, not unprecedented.
