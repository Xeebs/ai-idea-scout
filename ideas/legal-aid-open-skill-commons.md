# LegalAid Commons: A Multi-Org-Governed, Subscription-Free Open Skill Library for Civil Legal Aid

- **Status**: PROPOSED
- **Date**: 2026-11-12
- **Topic**: Applied AI for underserved domains
- **Source**: research/2026-11-12-applied-ai-for-underserved-domains.md · P7
- **Maturity verdict**: PARTIAL — LawDroid's Legal Aid Plugin (May 2026, ~15 skills, open-source) is real and free, but one analysis flags open maintenance/security sustainability as an open question and it still requires a Claude subscription; Stanford Legal Design Lab's co-pilots are pre-release; Free Law Project offers infrastructure (CourtListener API) but not an AI copilot itself. No broad, multi-org-adopted, subscription-independent ecosystem was found — a single ~6-month-old entrant, not a mature commons.
- **Format**: BOTH
- **Data Sources**: MANUAL at MVP — a legal-aid intake worker pastes the client's intake answers and the relevant program's published eligibility rules (a benefits agency's PDF or statute text); CONNECTED upgrade path exists via the Free Law Project's CourtListener API (mature, free, already serving case-law/docket data to nonprofits) for skills that need case-law context beyond a bounded benefits-eligibility ruleset.

## The Gap
LawDroid's Legal Aid Plugin was built, per its own coverage, because "the Claude-pocalypse bypassed legal aid" — i.e. mainstream AI legal tooling wasn't reaching this population until one plugin arrived in May 2026. But that plugin still requires a Claude subscription (a real cost barrier for the smallest legal-aid orgs this digest's P5 pattern separately flags as priced out of AI tooling generally), and an independent analysis flags its single-maintainer open-source sustainability as unresolved. Queen's University's OpenJustice and Stanford's Legal Design Lab are both still building pre-release "reference architectures," not shipped, adopted tools. The pattern across every source: one early, subscription-dependent entrant, academic efforts not yet shipped, and no multi-org-governed, subscription-free alternative.

## The Project
An open skill library for civil legal-aid work — intake triage, benefits/housing eligibility assessment, and plain-language form drafting — explicitly designed to run on free or low-cost model access (a self-hosted open-weight model, or any provider's free tier a legal-aid org already qualifies for) rather than requiring a paid Claude/ChatGPT seat, and governed by a small council of 3-5 founding legal-aid orgs rather than a single maintainer. Each skill ships with a published evaluation protocol (a labeled test set of real-shaped intake scenarios with known-correct outcomes, scored before every release) so adopting orgs can verify quality themselves rather than trusting an unaudited plugin. The governance model — not just the skills — is the centerpiece: a shared release/review process that survives any one maintainer leaving, the specific sustainability risk flagged against LawDroid.

## Tangible Impact
**Population**: Civil legal-aid organizations and their pro bono attorney networks serving low-income clients — the Legal Services Corporation estimates roughly 92% of low-income Americans' civil legal needs go unmet or under-met annually, and the specific population this targets is the smallest, least-resourced legal-aid orgs who can build or afford neither a bespoke chatbot (like Navi or LIA) nor an ongoing Claude subscription for an existing plugin.
**Mechanism**: Today, a small legal-aid org without subscription budget or in-house engineering either does without AI assistance entirely or adopts an unaudited, single-maintainer tool whose long-term sustainability is already flagged as uncertain. A subscription-free, multi-org-governed library removes the recurring-cost barrier and gives each adopting org a published quality bar (the evaluation protocol) instead of an unverifiable "trust us" plugin.
**Measure**: (1) number of distinct legal-aid orgs actively using at least one skill from the library after 6 months, tracked as an adoption proxy for the real multi-org reach LawDroid's single-plugin model lacks; (2) precision/recall of each skill's eligibility or intake-triage output against its published labeled test set, re-measured every release; (3) the library's continuity across a maintainer transition (does a release still ship on schedule if the original founding org steps back) — the direct test of the governance model's sustainability claim.

## Why Now
LSC's continued Technology Initiative Grant funding for legal-aid AI tooling (the same funding line behind Navi and LIA) and LawDroid's May 2026 launch both confirm active demand and prove the skill-authoring pattern works technically — the open window right now is specifically the governance and subscription-independence gap LawDroid's own coverage names as unresolved, before a single subscription-gated plugin becomes the de facto standard simply by being first.

## Skill Angle
The library itself is a set of individually-invokable skills — e.g., a benefits-eligibility-intake-triage skill that takes a client's pasted intake answers and a program's eligibility rules and returns a structured eligibility assessment with flagged uncertain items. Each skill is independently testable and adoptable by a single org before that org commits to the full governed-library relationship; the project is the persistent infrastructure around them — the shared repo, the release/review governance process, and the published per-skill evaluation protocol that must be re-run and re-published on every change.

## MVP Sketch
Scope to one sub-vertical first: benefits-eligibility intake triage for one or two specific programs (e.g., SNAP or housing-voucher eligibility in 1-2 states, where rules are a bounded, enumerable set — the same scoping VerifyAid's verification-workflow idea uses for the same reason). Recruit 2-3 founding legal-aid org partners to co-govern the first release and provide real (de-identified) past intake cases for the evaluation protocol. Stack: a self-hostable open-weight model or free-tier API call, a simple eligibility-rules lookup, and a shared GitHub repo with a lightweight release-review process. The validating experiment: ship one skill, have each founding org independently score it against the published evaluation protocol on their own past cases, and confirm scores are consistent across orgs before adding a second skill.

## Risks & Open Questions
- Recruiting even 3 legal-aid orgs into a shared governance commitment is itself a non-trivial organizing effort, separate from any engineering risk — this needs direct outreach validation before the build, not after.
- Legal-aid orgs are liability-sensitive; any eligibility-assessment skill needs an explicit scope disclaimer and probably a legal review of the tool itself, mirroring the caution already flagged for VerifyAid's narrower verification tool.
- Risk that LawDroid's plugin, despite the subscription dependency, already has enough community momentum that a parallel effort fragments adoption rather than filling a real gap — worth a direct compatibility/complementarity check (could this library's skills run as LawDroid-compatible modules?) before building a fully separate stack.
- Free/self-hosted model quality may lag what a paid Claude subscription delivers on nuanced eligibility edge cases — the evaluation protocol needs to honestly surface where the subscription-free approach loses, not just where it saves cost.
