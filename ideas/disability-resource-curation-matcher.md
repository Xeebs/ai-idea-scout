# Found For Me: An AI-Matched Resource Curation Layer for Disability Profiles

- **Status**: PROPOSED
- **Date**: 2026-11-14
- **Topic**: Applied AI for underserved domains
- **Source**: research/2026-11-14-applied-ai-for-underserved-domains.md · P6
- **Maturity verdict**: PARTIAL — gh search for "disability resource matching recommendation" and "assistive technology recommender" returned zero repos. WebSearch found only research-stage/unshipped efforts: "Buddy" (academic AI recommender matching cognitive-disability users to AT parameters via a questionnaire, published in a research chapter, no public deployment) and Qatar's Mada Center "challenge statement" seeking exactly this system (an open call, not a built product) — no general, deployed, cross-disability resource-curation layer exists.
- **Format**: BOTH
- **Data Sources**: MANUAL — the person seeking resources describes their disability profile and context in their own words (e.g. "I have dyslexia and ADHD, I'm trying to read a 300-page textbook for a class"), optionally pasting or uploading an existing diagnosis/accommodation letter for more precise matching. The matched-against resource database is itself community-curated (populated by submissions, ratings, and reviews from disabled users and advocates, not read live from an external API) — no mature external API for this kind of disability-resource directory was confirmed to exist today (AbleData, the historical U.S. federal directory, is defunct; its successor, the AT3 Center, does not expose a documented public API), so a CONNECTED version is noted as a future upgrade path, not available at launch.

## The Gap
A poster on r/accessibility described building their own personal list of resources after realizing mainstream reading recommendations assumed "a typical way of doing things" didn't fit their specific disabilities (r/accessibility, "Reading My Own Way: Sharing Resources for People with Disabilities", 2026-11-13). This is distinct from a previously-covered pattern about disabled people building new assistive tech from scratch — this is about discovering and matching *existing* tools and resources (apps, techniques, services, communities) to a specific, individual disability profile, a curation/matching gap that stays manual and informal today because mainstream recommendation engines assume a "typical" user.

## The Project
An intake-and-match system: a person describes their disability profile and the specific task or life domain they need help with (reading, employment, parenting, navigating benefits, etc.), and the system matches them against a community-curated, continuously-rated database of resources — specific apps, techniques, services, and peer communities — tagged by which disability profiles and contexts they actually work for, based on real user reviews rather than a vendor's marketing claim. The core differentiator from a generic search engine or forum is the matching logic: an LLM reads the free-text profile description and the task, maps it against the tagged resource corpus, and explains *why* each suggested resource fits this specific combination (not just "popular resources for dyslexia" generically) — the same personalized-fit judgment the original Reddit poster was doing manually for themselves and sharing informally.

## Tangible Impact
**Population**: People with reading/writing and related disabilities trying to find tools or techniques that fit their specific profile rather than a generic "typical" recommendation — a large population (the National Center for Learning Disabilities estimates roughly 1 in 5 Americans has a learning and/or attention disability), with the Reddit poster's own described experience as direct first-person evidence of the unmet need.
**Mechanism**: Replaces the current default — a disabled person manually trial-and-erroring through generic, "typical-user" resource lists, or relying on word-of-mouth from their own small network — with a matching layer that surfaces resources other people with a similar specific profile have actually rated as working, cutting the time and frustration of finding a fit and surfacing options the person wouldn't have found through a generic search.
**Measure**: (1) self-reported time-to-find-a-working-resource compared to the person's prior search method; (2) resource-rating follow-through rate (did the matched resource actually work for this person, reported back into the system); (3) database growth and repeat-usage rate as a proxy for whether the curation layer earns ongoing trust from the community it serves.

## Why Now
LLMs are now good enough to read an unstructured, highly individual disability-profile description and map it meaningfully against a tagged resource corpus — a matching task that previously required either rigid structured-questionnaire systems (like the academic "Buddy" recommender) or pure human peer-to-peer sharing (like the Reddit poster's own approach), and can now sit between those two extremes without losing the nuance of a free-text description.

## Skill Angle
A companion skill takes a single free-text disability-profile-plus-task description and, given a bundled or user-supplied seed list of community-reviewed resources (rather than the full live database), produces a short, explained shortlist of fits — usable standalone by someone who wants a one-off recommendation without the full platform existing yet, and a way to validate the matching logic's quality before committing to building and maintaining the full curated database.

## MVP Sketch
Scope: seed a database with ~50-100 resources manually curated from existing disability-community recommendation threads (starting with the original Reddit poster's own list and similar threads), each tagged by disability type, task domain, and a short note on who it worked for. Build a simple intake form + LLM-matching layer that returns a ranked, explained shortlist. Rough stack: a small Postgres/Airtable-backed resource store, a matching prompt pipeline, a minimal web form. Single experiment: have 15-20 people from r/accessibility and related disability communities submit their own profile+task and rate whether the top-3 matched resources were genuinely more relevant to their specific situation than what a generic web search for their disability type returned.

## Risks & Open Questions
- Cold-start problem: the matching quality is only as good as the seed database, which starts small and risks over-representing whichever disability communities contribute first.
- Free-text disability-profile descriptions touch sensitive personal health information; the project needs a clear, explicit data-handling and consent policy before any pilot, not as an afterthought.
- Risk of duplicating the existing (but non-public-API) AT3 Center federal resource directory's mission rather than complementing it — worth exploring a partnership or data-sharing conversation with AT3 Center rather than building a fully parallel database from scratch.
- "Resource works for this profile" is inherently subjective and context-dependent; the rating/matching mechanism needs guardrails against a small number of vocal reviewers skewing results for an entire disability category.
