# No Entiendo, Flagged: A Confidence and Escalation Gate for Indigenous-Language AI Translation

- **Status**: EXPLORING (promoted 2026-11-13 cycle 61: evidence density — ≥2 independent threads, EdSource's reporting on Salinas Valley Mixteco/Zapotec parents, the grupoanimal.mx chatbot-test report documenting "no entiendo" failures, the Middlebury Institute piece on interpreter-coverage gaps, and academic BLEU data confirming inconsistent MT quality, all converging on the same translation-quality gap)
- **Date**: 2026-11-13
- **Topic**: Applied AI for underserved domains
- **Source**: research/2026-11-13-applied-ai-underserved-domains-6th.md · P6
- **Maturity verdict**: PARTIAL — WebSearch found Google Translate added Zapotec, Mixtec, and Nahuatl (2025 rollout) and ModelFront advertises "full support" for both — shipped, maintained products exist. But academic MT literature on Mixtec-Spanish shows wildly inconsistent quality by domain/corpus (BLEU ~17-22 general corpus vs. ~96-99 narrow educational/religious-text corpus) and INALI counts 81 Mixtec variants, 50+ Zapotec languages, often differing town-to-town — exactly the mismatch producing the "no entiendo" chatbot failures. GitHub search for "Mixtec Zapotec translation" / "indigenous language machine translation low resource" returned zero results.
- **Format**: BOTH
- **Data Sources**: CONNECTED — wraps existing mature MT APIs (Google Translate's 2025 Mixtec/Zapotec/Nahuatl endpoints, ModelFront) via their public API to perform the base translation, layering a confidence/escalation check and a community-sourced glossary on top. Google Translate's indigenous-language API support is newly mature enough (2025 rollout) to build on directly rather than defaulting to MANUAL; the glossary/escalation-rule data itself is community-contributed, fed through a structured manual-curation pipeline rather than pulled from any external source.

## The Gap
EdSource (March 2026) reports Triqui- and Mixteco-speaking parents in Greenfield, CA (Salinas Valley) saying they can't advocate for their children without interpreters; the district partly relies on Nurbli, an AI-based over-the-phone interpretation app. State data counts a severe undercount of students speaking these languages (7,681 Mixteco speakers, 374 Zapotec speakers statewide, 2024-25). A September 2026 Mexican report (grupoanimal.mx) found Zapotec and Mixtec speakers testing chatbots got responses that repeated the same terms or flatly said "no entiendo" — a failure mode speakers said wouldn't occur in Spanish. Every mitigation found (Nurbli, Indigenous Interpreting+) is a thin, partial stopgap explicitly described as struggling with coverage or quality; no tool closes the quality gap between Spanish and indigenous-language AI translation, or even makes the gap visible to the person relying on it.

## The Project
A gateway that sits in front of existing MT APIs (Google Translate, ModelFront) for a given translation request, flags when the output is likely unreliable for the specific requested variant — using known failure signatures (repeated-term loops, literal "no entiendo" responses, low API-reported confidence, mismatch against a community-sourced glossary of common school/health phrases) — and either routes to a glossary-corrected answer or flags for human-interpreter escalation (e.g. via Indigenous Interpreting+) instead of silently returning a degraded answer with the same apparent confidence as the Spanish output. The confidence-scoring and escalation logic, plus the maintained per-variant glossary, is the centerpiece; it deliberately does not try to out-build Google's or ModelFront's underlying MT models.

## Tangible Impact
**Population**: indigenous-language-speaking farmworkers and their parent/student family members interacting with AI-mediated translation in school and health settings. Same CA state data as above — specifically the Greenfield/Salinas Valley families EdSource cites who say they can't advocate for their children without reliable interpretation, within a national population the National Center for Farmworker Health estimates at several hundred thousand indigenous-language speakers.
**Mechanism**: detects the documented "no entiendo"/repeated-term failure mode before it reaches the parent or health-visit participant, and routes to a community-validated correction or a human interpreter instead of letting a degraded translation masquerade as equally reliable to the Spanish output — closing the specific visibility gap, not just the raw quality gap, that the grupoanimal.mx report's interviewees flagged.
**Measure**: (1) reduction in silent-failure rate ("no entiendo"/repeated-term responses reaching the end user) on a held-out test set of common school/health phrases per target variant; (2) precision/recall of the escalation flag against a hand-labeled set of known-bad translations; (3) parent/advocate-reported trust in translated guidance, collected via a partner school-district pilot (e.g. Greenfield Union School District, the EdSource-cited case).

## Why Now
Google Translate's 2025 addition of Mixtec/Zapotec/Nahuatl support is recent enough that no one has yet built a reliability layer on top of it, and the academic BLEU-score evidence (17-22 general vs. 96-99 narrow-corpus) plus the September 2026 grupoanimal.mx report give concrete, current failure signatures to build the confidence rules from rather than guessing.

## Skill Angle
Trigger: a school staffer, health worker, or community-org volunteer has a specific phrase or short document that needs translating into a target indigenous-language variant and wants to know whether to trust the MT output before using it. Inputs: the source text, the target variant, and the context (school notice, health instruction). Output: the MT translation plus a confidence flag and, if flagged, a community-glossary-corrected alternative or an explicit "recommend human interpreter" note — a one-shot risk-check using a bundled starter glossary for 2-3 priority variants, distinct from the project's live, continuously-updated gateway service sitting in front of the MT API in production.

## MVP Sketch
Partner with Indigenous Interpreting+ or a Salinas Valley school district to build an initial glossary of 100-200 common school/health phrases validated in 1-2 variants (e.g. the specific Mixteco variant used in Greenfield schools), wire a thin layer over the Google Translate API that checks output against known failure signatures plus the glossary, and benchmark escalation precision/recall against a hand-labeled test set of 50-100 real or representative phrases. 3-4 weeks, 1-2 people plus the partner org's native-speaker review time.

## Risks & Open Questions
- Dialect fragmentation (81 Mixtec variants, 50+ Zapotec languages per INALI) means a glossary validated for one town's variant may not generalize; the MVP needs to scope and claim coverage narrowly, matching the real variant spoken in the pilot community.
- A confidence gate is only as good as its failure-signature list; genuinely novel failure modes not yet observed will still slip through as false negatives.
- Strongest reason this might already be solved: Nurbli or Indigenous Interpreting+ could already have an internal confidence-scoring step not publicly documented — worth direct outreach before assuming greenfield.
- Over-flagging erodes trust and defeats the purpose if every translation gets escalated; thresholds need real pilot tuning against the partner org's actual usage patterns, not a one-size rule.
