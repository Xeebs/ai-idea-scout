# Access By Default: An Open Standard and Reference Implementation for Accessible AI Hiring Pipelines

- **Status**: PROPOSED
- **Date**: 2026-11-20
- **Topic**: Applied AI for underserved domains
- **Source**: research/2026-11-20-applied-ai-for-underserved-domains.md · P7
- **Maturity verdict**: PARTIAL — guidance exists (WCAG 2.2 baseline, the DOL/ODEP/EARN/PEAT eRecruiting checklist) and one vendor claim exists (Sapia.ai's chat-based, untimed interview format marketed as fairer for disabled candidates), but there is no vendor-independent test data, no certification standard, and no counter-example of a comprehensively accessible-by-default AI hiring pipeline. AFB's own March 2026 "The AI Quagmire" report frames this as an open, unexplored risk area, and that framing held up under search.
- **Format**: BOTH
- **Data Sources**: MANUAL — a submitting vendor or employer uploads or describes their interview/assessment flow (screenshots, a flow description, or a sandboxed test account) for independent evaluation against the standard; actual test results are recorded by human disabled testers using their own real assistive technology, which cannot be automated or connected to an API, since the entire point is a real person's real-assistive-tech judgment, not a fetchable signal.

## The Gap
AFB's March 2026 report found more disabled than nondisabled job seekers reported difficulty with automated tests and interviews, including having to turn off their own assistive technology just to use the AI system, and states plainly that accessibility-barrier risks from AI hiring tools "have not yet been fully explored." The only counter-evidence found is Sapia.ai's self-marketed, vendor-reported claim of a fairer, chat-based, untimed format — with no independent test data behind it. Existing guidance (WCAG 2.2, the DOL/ODEP/EARN/PEAT checklist) tells a builder what to aim for in the abstract, but no one has turned that guidance into a testable standard plus a working example that actually passes it.

This differs from this repo's existing `ai-hiring-screen-applicant-defense-kit` idea in both actor and timing: that project is an applicant-side tool helping an individual disabled job seeker cope with or dispute an already-opaque, already-built pipeline after the fact. This project targets the vendor or employer building or buying the pipeline, aiming to prevent the barrier at its source rather than help an applicant route around one that already exists.

## The Project
Two linked deliverables. First, a published, testable accessibility standard for AI hiring/interview tools specifically — not generic WCAG, but the hiring-specific failure modes AFB documented and the checklist orgs name: a required non-timed alternative to any timed assessment, a required equivalent to camera/video-based analysis for candidates who can't or won't use one, assistive-tech compatibility verified during live interaction (not just static page scan), and a plain-language accommodation-request flow built into the product itself. Second, an open-source reference interview/assessment flow that actually passes the standard, validated by disabled job-seeker testers using their own real screen readers, switch access, and speech-recognition setups — giving HR-tech vendors something concrete and triable to build against instead of only abstract guidance, and giving advocacy orgs and procurement officers an independent test they can run against any vendor's real product to check self-reported claims like Sapia.ai's.

## Tangible Impact
**Population**: The roughly 61 million U.S. adults with a disability (CDC) who are job seekers encountering automated hiring screens, where AFB's own survey found this group reports measurably higher friction than nondisabled job seekers on the same tools; secondarily, HR-tech vendors and employers who currently have no credible, checkable way to substantiate an accessibility claim.
**Mechanism**: Today an employer buying or building an AI hiring tool has no independent test to know whether it's actually usable with real assistive technology, so vendors self-report without evidence (as Sapia.ai does) while disabled candidates keep hitting the exact disable-your-own-assistive-technology barrier AFB documented. An open standard plus a passing reference implementation gives vendors something concrete to build against and gives advocacy organizations and procurement officers an independent test to verify any vendor's claim before it's trusted.
**Measure**: (1) task-completion rate and time for disabled testers using their own real assistive technology on the reference implementation vs. a baseline mainstream AI hiring tool; (2) number of vendors whose product is independently tested against the standard, and the pass/fail breakdown by criterion; (3) rate of "had to disable assistive technology" incidents reported by testers, the exact AFB-documented failure mode, before vs. after a vendor adopts the standard.

## Why Now
AFB's March 2026 report gives this project a fresh, citable, and specific failure taxonomy to build the standard's criteria from rather than guessing, and the existing DOL/ODEP/EARN/PEAT checklist already did the groundwork of naming hiring-specific accessibility concerns — the missing piece is turning that into something testable with a working example behind it, not inventing the concerns from scratch.

## Skill Angle
A companion skill: given a description of a planned or existing AI hiring/interview flow, the skill drafts a gap report against the standard's checklist — naming exactly which required evidence is missing (e.g., "no stated non-timed alternative to the assessment," "no documented assistive-tech compatibility testing," "no in-product accommodation-request path") before a team submits for formal independent evaluation. This is the one-time self-assessment aid; the project itself is the ongoing independent-tester evaluation program and the public standard/registry it maintains across many vendors over time.

## MVP Sketch
Compile the standard's first version directly from the DOL/ODEP/EARN/PEAT checklist plus AFB's documented failure modes (4-6 testable criteria to start: non-timed alternative, assistive-tech live-interaction compatibility, in-product accommodation path, camera-analysis opt-out equivalence). Build one minimal open-source reference flow — a simple untimed, chat-based mock interview tool — that deliberately satisfies all of them. Recruit 4-6 disabled testers using their own real assistive technology to attempt both the reference flow and a mainstream comparison tool, and compare task completion and self-reported friction. Stack: a basic web interview flow, a published checklist document, and a small structured test-report template. 2-4 weeks, 1-2 people plus the testers' time.

## Risks & Open Questions
- Sapia.ai or another vendor may already have internal accessibility test data they haven't published — worth direct outreach before assuming no vendor has done any real testing.
- A standard with no legal or procurement-mandated teeth risks being ignored by vendors who don't already want this work done; distribution strategy (e.g., pitching it to a procurement body or disability-employment nonprofit as a requirement) matters as much as the standard's content.
- Recruiting and fairly compensating disabled testers across multiple assistive-technology types is a real, ongoing operational cost, not a one-time MVP expense.
- "Accessible-by-default" may conflict with other hiring-tool design goals (e.g., anti-cheating measures built around timed tests or camera monitoring) in ways that need real vendor conversations to resolve, not just a checklist assertion.
