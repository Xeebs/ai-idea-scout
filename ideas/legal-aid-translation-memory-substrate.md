# Legal Lexicon: A Shared, Open Translation-Memory Substrate for Multilingual Legal-Aid Assistants

- **Status**: EXPLORING
- **Date**: 2026-11-17
- **Topic**: Applied AI for underserved domains
- **Source**: research/2026-11-17-applied-ai-for-underserved-domains.md · P6
- **Maturity verdict**: OPEN — Stanford's Legal Design Lab, the organization closest to this problem, explicitly states its own shared "Translation Memory database" for legal help has only "started gathering glossaries that already exist," i.e. named as unbuilt by the domain expert itself. The EU's DGT-TM/IATE (EU-law-focused) and a 2012 Japanese law-translation paper are the only tangential prior art, neither scoped to legal aid. Independent point-solution legal chatbots (LawGlance, "ai-legal-assistant") exist with no shared terminology layer to draw from.
- **Format**: BOTH
- **Data Sources**: MANUAL (content contribution) — legal-aid staff and certified/community interpreters paste verified term-pairs, example sentences, and jurisdiction/practice-area tags into a moderated submission form; seeded in bulk at launch from existing public legal glossaries (e.g., EU IATE/DGT-TM entries filtered for overlapping terms, state court self-help-center glossaries). The project's own output then becomes a new CONNECTED resource — a queryable API/MCP server any legal-aid assistant can read from — but that connector doesn't exist today and is itself part of what this idea builds.

## The Gap
Multiple independent multilingual legal-aid chatbots exist (LawGlance, a separate "ai-legal-assistant" repo, plus the chatbots VerifyAid's companion idea already documents — Lone Star Legal Aid's Navi, NC Legal Aid's LIA, Hong Kong's PoBot), each presumably building its own ad hoc translation and legal-terminology handling. Stanford's Legal Design Lab proposed the obvious fix — a shared, open translation-memory database for legal help — but its own public writeup says the effort has only begun gathering existing glossaries. This is the rare case where the people closest to the problem have confirmed, in writing, that the shared substrate doesn't exist yet, while the point-solution chatbots needing it keep multiplying.

## The Project
An open, community-maintained translation-memory and legal-terminology-glossary database, scoped specifically to legal-aid contexts (as opposed to EU commercial/statutory law) — term-pairs across language pairs, each tagged with jurisdiction, practice area (housing, family, immigration, benefits), and a disambiguating example sentence, since legal terms like "eviction," "garnishment," or "ex parte" have precise meanings that a generic MT system routinely flattens or mistranslates. The database is exposed as a queryable API/MCP connector any legal-aid chatbot can call instead of building its own translation layer, returning the vetted term and its example usage rather than a bare dictionary lookup. A moderation workflow (legal-aid staff and interpreters review and approve submissions before they go live) keeps the substrate trustworthy enough for real client-facing use, picking up directly from where the Legal Design Lab's glossary-gathering stopped.

## Tangible Impact
**Population**: The estimated 26 million U.S. residents with limited English proficiency (Census ACS data) who seek legal-aid help in a language other than English, and the independent legal-aid chatbot projects (LawGlance, Navi, LIA, PoBot, and any future entrant) currently each building their own translation/terminology layer from scratch with no shared substrate to draw from.
**Mechanism**: Today a legal-aid assistant either skips multilingual support, relies on generic MT that mistranslates precise legal terms, or hand-builds its own glossary in isolation — work the Legal Design Lab itself confirms nobody has shared yet. A queryable shared substrate lets any adopting tool return a vetted, jurisdiction-aware translation for a legal term instead of a generic MT guess, and lets a new multilingual legal-aid tool launch without first re-deriving a glossary other teams already built.
**Measure**: (1) number of distinct legal-aid tools/orgs querying the substrate in production after 6 months (adoption, the direct test of whether "shared substrate" demand is real); (2) term-translation accuracy on a held-out set of legal-aid-specific terms, benchmarked against generic MT on the same set; (3) glossary coverage growth (number of validated term-pairs x language-pair x practice-area combinations) over time, as the Legal Design Lab's own "started gathering" effort is the current baseline to beat.

## Why Now
The Legal Design Lab's own 2026 writeup is a direct, recent admission that this substrate is needed and not yet built, and multiple independent multilingual legal-aid chatbots are already live and would be immediate adopters of a shared layer instead of each maintaining its own ad hoc translation code — the demand and the gap are both freshly confirmed in the same digest window.

## Skill Angle
A companion skill drafts a legal-aid translation for a given phrase/document and target language using the shared glossary: given a piece of legal text and the target language, it returns a draft translation that explicitly flags any term not yet in the verified glossary (instead of silently guessing), so a legal-aid staff member or volunteer interpreter can use it as a first-pass draft with clear visibility into what still needs human verification. This differs from the project itself — the skill is a one-shot drafting aid for a human reviewer; the project is the persistent, queryable database and API other software calls directly.

## MVP Sketch
Scope to one language pair and two practice areas first (e.g., Spanish legal-aid terminology for housing and family law, the highest-volume LEP need per most legal-aid intake data) and recruit 2-3 of the existing legal-aid chatbot projects (LawGlance, or a Navi/LIA-adjacent team) as founding data contributors and API consumers. Stack: a Postgres-backed term-pair database with jurisdiction/practice-area tags, a simple moderation queue for submissions, and a thin REST/MCP API wrapping lookups. Single validating experiment: have one partner chatbot swap its existing ad hoc Spanish housing-law translation handling for the shared API and measure whether translation accuracy on a held-out legal-term test set improves.

## Risks & Open Questions
- Legal terminology correctness has real liability consequences if a translated term is wrong in a client-facing context; the moderation/verification step must be a hard gate, not optional, before any term is served live.
- Recruiting legal-aid chatbot teams to actually integrate against a new shared API (rather than keep their own working translation code) is an adoption problem as much as a technical one — needs direct partner commitment validated before the full build.
- The Legal Design Lab may already be further along internally than its public writeup suggests; a direct outreach check is warranted before building a fully separate, possibly duplicative substrate.
- Scoping to legal-aid-specific terminology (vs. general legal translation) is the whole value proposition here — if practice-area/jurisdiction tagging turns out too coarse to disambiguate real terms, the substrate risks collapsing back into generic MT with extra metadata.
