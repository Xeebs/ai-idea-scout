# Reject First: A Packaged Community-Verdict-Then-Co-Design Protocol for Underserved-Health AI Tools

- **Status**: PROPOSED
- **Date**: 2026-11-20
- **Topic**: Applied AI for underserved domains
- **Source**: research/2026-11-20-applied-ai-for-underserved-domains.md · P5
- **Maturity verdict**: PARTIAL — confirmed as a single documented, real, and RCT-evaluated case (Springer Nature "Building a primary care chatbot with people, not for them," China, 2,100+ RCT participants), but reported as a one-off case study, not packaged as a repeatable protocol or toolkit. Two further targeted searches found no generalized "reject-first co-design protocol" anywhere else.
- **Format**: BOTH
- **Data Sources**: MANUAL — the project team fills in structured templates (existing-tool evaluation session notes, the documented reject/accept verdict, co-design workshop minutes, infra-stress-test results) through a lightweight tracking tool; no external API or connector exists for "did this specific community actually reject this specific existing tool," because that verdict is a one-time qualitative judgment the community itself has to render, not data any system already holds.

## The Gap
A Springer Nature case study describes a team building a primary-care chatbot who first asked community members whether existing AI health tools would help — and "got a clear 'no'" — then found their own early prototype "unusable" on local networks before rebuilding with a tiered/offline backbone alongside resident co-designers, a sequence later validated in an RCT of 2,100+ participants. This is a genuinely different order of operations than most AI-for-underserved-health projects follow (build first, consult later, if at all), and it worked. But it exists today only as a narrative case study inside one paper. No toolkit, checklist, or repeatable protocol packages this sequence for the next team to actually follow — a search for exactly this (a packaged "reject-first co-design protocol") found only unrelated co-design studies in different populations (LGBTQ+ teen sexual health, rural mental-wellbeing chatbots), none offering the same structured, build-blocking verdict-gate.

This differs in scope and mechanism from this repo's existing `assistive-ai-codesign-governance-protocol` idea: that project is a cross-disability, post-hoc certification registry reviewed by disabled community reviewers, checking a finished or in-progress product's evidence of genuine co-design after the fact. This project is a pre-build, sequential process methodology — document an explicit reject verdict on existing tools *before* writing any code, then co-design, then specifically re-test core infrastructure assumptions against what that process surfaced — aimed at general underserved-health-AI teams, not assistive technology for a specific disability vertical, and not a certifying body.

## The Project
A reusable protocol plus a lightweight tracking tool, built directly from the one validated case: (1) a structured "existing-tool evaluation session" script that elicits an explicit accept/reject verdict from community members on tools already available to them, not vague open-ended feedback; (2) a hard decision gate — a team cannot mark the "build" phase started in the tracking tool until that verdict is documented; (3) a co-design workshop template that puts residents in an active-design role (not a consulted-after-the-fact role), mirroring the China case's resident-co-designer structure; (4) an infrastructure-reality checklist prompting teams to re-test their core technical assumptions (connectivity, device, literacy) against what the evaluation session actually surfaced, the exact step that caught the China team's prototype being "unusable" before it shipped; (5) a simple tracking app recording evidence against each gate so a funder or reviewer can verify the sequence was actually followed, not just claimed.

## Tangible Impact
**Population**: Teams — NGOs, academic health informatics groups, and startups — building AI health tools for rural and underserved communities, and downstream, the primary-care populations they serve; the Springer Nature case alone validated this against 2,100+ rural participants in China, within a global rural primary-care population the WHO counts in the hundreds of millions across low- and middle-income countries.
**Mechanism**: Teams that skip a structured community-evaluation step risk building a tool the community has already implicitly or explicitly rejected, as the China team discovered before rebuilding; a protocol that forces the reject-first verdict and the infrastructure-reality check earlier in the build cycle front-loads the two failure modes (wrong trust assumption, wrong infra assumption) the case study shows can sink a project, increasing the odds of the kind of real-world uptake the RCT measured.
**Measure**: (1) adoption/uptake rate of tools built following the protocol vs. a matched baseline of comparable tools that skipped a documented community-evaluation step; (2) engineering time/resources spent on a build later abandoned or substantially reworked after late-stage community rejection, before vs. after teams adopt the protocol; (3) number of documented "reject" verdicts captured and demonstrably acted on (a proxy for whether teams are genuinely doing the step, not checking a box).

## Why Now
The China case's RCT result is recent enough to be directly citable evidence that the reject-first sequence works, not just a plausible-sounding idea, and no one has packaged it yet — the methodology risk is retired by one real, well-evaluated deployment; only the "make it repeatable for the next team" step is missing.

## Skill Angle
A companion skill: given a team's project description (target community, health domain, current build stage), the skill drafts a tailored existing-tool evaluation session script and an infrastructure-reality checklist specific to their stated deployment context — the one-time planning aid a team runs before or during early design. This differs from the project, which is the persistent, cross-project tracking tool and the protocol templates themselves, refined over many projects' documented outcomes rather than generated once per team.

## MVP Sketch
Scope to one willing pilot team about to start (not mid-build on) an AI health tool for an underserved community. Build the three core templates (evaluation-session script, co-design workshop agenda, infra-stress-test checklist) plus a minimal tracking tool (a shared doc or simple Airtable-backed form is enough for v1) enforcing the sequence. Stack: static templates, a lightweight web form for evidence capture, no ML required at this stage — the protocol itself is the product. Validating experiment: run the pilot team through the full sequence and compare their documented decision points against the China case's reported sequence — did the evaluation session surface a real accept/reject verdict, and did the infra checklist catch anything the team would otherwise have missed until later.

## Risks & Open Questions
- One validated case is a real result but a small evidence base to generalize a protocol from — the pilot needs to genuinely test whether the sequence transfers to a different community/health domain, not assume it does.
- A hard decision gate that blocks "starting the build" risks teams simply working around it (building anyway, documenting after the fact) unless there's a real incentive (funder requirement, institutional review) to follow it honestly.
- The China team's own researchers may already be generalizing their own methodology for other projects — worth direct outreach before building a parallel version.
- Recruiting a genuinely willing pilot team early enough in their process (before they've already committed to a build) is a real sourcing bottleneck, not just a design one.
