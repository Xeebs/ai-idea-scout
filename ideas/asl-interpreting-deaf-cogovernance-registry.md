## The Gap
Nagish, Sorenson, and Silence Speaks are all shipping or piloting real-time AI ASL interpreting explicitly framed as solving the rural/low-access interpreter shortage — Nagish's own CEO frames the value as helping people "where interpreters are hard to get." But a global survey of Deaf/HoH community members on sign-language technologies found the top two concerns were "hearing people making money from sign language technologies" and "few deaf people leading or contributing," and r/ASLinterpreters moderators are actively lobbying against the automated-interpreting timeline. The access promise and Deaf-led accountability are being decoupled, and nothing independently verifies which vendors have actually closed that gap versus just claiming to.

## The Project
A public, Deaf-led registry that scores AI ASL interpreting vendors against a concrete co-governance bar — majority-Deaf advisory authority (not just an advisory committee that can be overruled), published decision-making power over product changes, and a track record of acting on Deaf-reviewer feedback — built from the existing EUD ethical framework and the Cambridge/Minderoo procurement report rather than inventing a new rubric from scratch. Each vendor (Nagish, Sorenson, Silence Speaks, DeepMind's SL2T, Signapse) gets a public scorecard with cited evidence, re-reviewed on a fixed cadence as vendors update their governance claims. The centerpiece is the independently-applied, Deaf-reviewer-staffed scoring process itself — not a new interpreting model.

## Tangible Impact
**Population**: Deaf and hard-of-hearing people being offered AI ASL interpreting as a substitute for human interpreters in rural or low-access settings, and the institutional buyers (school districts, hospitals, courts) deciding which vendor to adopt on their behalf. ~11 million US adults report serious difficulty hearing (Census ACS), with interpreter shortages concentrated in exactly the rural counties these products target.
**Mechanism**: gives Deaf users and procurement teams a credible, independently-applied score instead of a vendor's self-reported "AI Sign Language Advisory Committee" press line, and gives vendors a concrete, public bar to meet for co-governance credibility — raising the cost of claiming Deaf involvement without ceding real authority.
**Measure**: (1) number of vendors scoring above the defined co-governance bar vs. self-reported-only claims; (2) Deaf-reviewer-rated trust/accuracy delta between certified and uncertified tools on a shared evaluation set; (3) adoption rate of certified tools among surveyed rural institutional buyers after the registry launches.

## Why Now
Two company-published governance frameworks (DeepMind's AISLAC, Signapse's) appeared within the last cycle of coverage, giving concrete claims to actually test against rather than a vacuum — and the same window produced the Deaf/HoH survey naming exactly the concern (hearing-people-profit, few-Deaf-leaders) this registry would directly surface and track.

## Skill Angle
Trigger: a procurement reviewer (school district AV/accessibility lead, hospital interpreter-services manager) is evaluating a specific AI ASL interpreting vendor and needs a fast, defensible answer on its governance claims. Inputs: the vendor's published product pages, any governance/advisory-board disclosures, and the evaluator's own notes from a sales call. Output: a structured pass/fail against each co-governance criterion (majority-Deaf authority, published decision power, responsiveness track record), with the specific evidence or gap cited for each — a one-shot procurement checklist distinct from the project's standing, continuously-reviewed public registry.

## MVP Sketch
Pick the EUD's 15-principle framework as the starting rubric, recruit 2-3 Deaf reviewers (via an existing Deaf advocacy org partnership) to score the 5 vendors named in the digest using only public information, and publish the first scorecard as a static site. 2-4 weeks, 1-2 people plus the volunteer Deaf reviewer panel.

## Risks & Open Questions
- Vendors could game a public-information-only score by publishing more impressive-sounding governance language without ceding real authority — the registry needs a way to flag "claims but no evidence of actual decision power."
- Deaf-reviewer capacity is scarce and valuable; a credible registry needs sustainable reviewer compensation, not volunteer burnout.
- Strongest reason this might already be solved: an existing Deaf advocacy organization (NAD, RID) could already be running something similar informally — worth direct outreach before assuming greenfield.
- A registry has no enforcement power beyond reputation; its impact depends entirely on buyers actually consulting it before procurement.
