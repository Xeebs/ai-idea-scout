# Quiet Channel: A Silent-Speech Input Adapter for Existing Voice-Agent Runtimes

Voice agents assume a mouth that can make sound. For a meaningful number of people — those who've had a laryngectomy, or who are living with ALS before full loss of bulbar control — that assumption is the whole product being inaccessible. The research needed to work around it already exists, in pieces, scattered across a Show HN launch and an MIT Media Lab wearable. Nobody has wired those pieces into the agent stacks people already use.

## The Gap

Two unrelated things surfaced independently in the same research run: a Show HN post called "Ramble," which lets you message your agent from an Apple Watch instead of speaking to it, and MIT Media Lab coverage of muscle-reading wearables that let you "use voice input without speaking a word." Neither is what it sounds like it should be. Ramble is a single-purpose weekend launch with three Hacker News points. The MIT work is hardware journalism, not a software development kit. Digging past both, the closest thing to a real implementation is SilentWear, an open-source textile neck-EMG interface out of ETH Zürich — genuinely usable code, with reported same-session accuracy of about 77.5%. But that number drops to 59.3% the moment you ask it to work on a different day than the one it was calibrated on. Other EMG and piezo wearables surfaced in the search (an MDPI 2025 Sensors paper, a hand-worn device out of Pepabo and Yokohama National University) are academic prototypes, not products. Nowhere in any of this is there a general-purpose layer that takes a silent-speech signal and feeds it into the voice-agent runtimes people are actually shipping today.

## The Project

The idea is deliberately narrow: don't build a new sensor, build the missing adapter. Take SilentWear's open EMG signal/inference pipeline as the input side, and plug its output into the text-turn APIs that LiveKit Agents and Pipecat already support as an alternative to live microphone audio — both runtimes accept this today, no new capability required there. The actual engineering center of gravity is a cross-session recalibration flow aimed squarely at SilentWear's own documented weak point: that 77.5%-to-59.3% falloff whenever a new session starts. If a short recalibration routine can meaningfully close that gap, you get something that was not possible before — EMG-based silent speech that reaches any voice-agent product already built on these runtimes, instead of a one-off integration per app per device.

Four pieces make this real: EMG signal ingestion forked from SilentWear's firmware and inference code, a per-session recalibration routine built specifically to attack the cross-session accuracy cliff, an intent-normalization layer that turns classified muscle signals into clean events, and thin adapters into LiveKit Agents' and Pipecat's existing text-turn input.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: People who cannot produce audible speech but retain residual muscle control sufficient for EMG-based silent speech — most directly, post-laryngectomy patients (the American Cancer Society estimates roughly 12,500 new laryngeal-cancer diagnoses per year in the U.S., a meaningful share of whom undergo laryngectomy) and people with ALS/motor neuron disease in earlier disease stages before full loss of bulbar/facial motor control (the ALS Association estimates roughly 30,000 Americans are living with ALS at any given time). Today this population has no way to use mainstream voice-agent products at all and is limited to slower AAC eye-gaze or switch-based text entry for anything requiring hands-free interaction.

**Mechanism**: A standard adapter means any voice-agent product a clinic, caregiver, or the person themselves already uses (a Vapi-built phone bot, a LiveKit-based assistant, a Pipecat voice app) becomes reachable via silent muscle signals instead of requiring a one-off bespoke integration per device and per app — the adapter is built once and reused across every downstream product that already speaks the text-turn protocol.

**Measure**: (1) successful command/intent recognition rate on a cross-session held-out test, tracked against SilentWear's own 59.3% cross-session baseline as the improvement target; (2) time-to-first-usable-command for a new user (minutes of recalibration, versus no path at all today since no integration exists); (3) number of distinct voice-agent platforms made reachable through one adapter instead of N bespoke one-off integrations.

## Try It

This one's a project, not a prompt — there's no companion Claude Code skill here. The MVP is concrete, though: fork SilentWear's open pipeline, train it on a small fixed vocabulary of 10–20 commands with a handful of volunteer testers, wire the classified intents into a basic Pipecat appointment-scheduling demo, and run the single experiment that matters — cross-session accuracy with and without the project's own recalibration step. That's the test that either validates this or kills it.

It's worth being upfront about the risks rather than papering over them. SilentWear's own numbers are the honest starting point, not a solved problem — closing that 77.5%-to-59.3% gap is a bet, unproven until someone actually tests it. Hardware access is a real bottleneck too: this needs a SilentWear-class EMG wearable or a partner who has one, and a software-only MVP can't validate the accuracy claims on its own. The target population also has varied residual-muscle profiles depending on condition and disease stage, so a one-size-fits-all classifier may need per-user tuning that adds friction this idea hasn't solved yet. And if a better-resourced AAC vendor like Tobii Dynavox or PRC-Saltillo ships an equivalent adapter first, the differentiation narrows fast to "open and hardware-agnostic" — worth watching, since that market is far more commercially organized than the hobbyist silent-input space this gap was found in.
