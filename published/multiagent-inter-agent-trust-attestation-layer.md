# Claim Ledger: A Signed Attestation and Trust-Scoring Layer for Inter-Agent Claims

Right now, when one AI agent tells another "I verified this," "tests passed," or "the payment cleared," the receiving agent just believes it. There's no signature, no audit trail, no record of whether that agent has been right before — just one LLM's word passed straight into another's next action. As agents start approving payments and skipping redundant checks based on a peer's self-report, that blind trust stops being a quirky implementation detail and starts being the thing that breaks.

## The Gap

The clearest signal is a Hacker News post, "Are we building multi-agent systems without a proper trust layer?" (4 points, 2026-11-14) — notable not for its popularity but for how it's framed: an open question nobody in the thread could answer. That complaint-shaped framing is the tell. A gap-analysis pass two days later, searching independently, landed on the exact same problem a second time, which is its own kind of evidence — this isn't one person's pet peeve, it's a gap two separate searches kept tripping over.

Digging into what already exists turns up a scatter of narrow, uncoordinated attempts rather than a solved problem: KYA (an Ed25519-signing, trust-scoring PyPI package backed by a self-reported arXiv paper), atrib (Ed25519 plus Merkle-log provenance on GitHub, roughly 5 stars), attest (an MCP artifact verifier that shipped with a hardcoded signing-key bug), Agent Manifest/TRACE (mentioned only in an awesome-list, nothing to actually run), ArkForge Trust Layer (a vendor's marketing-stage proxy), and agentmem (a GitHub project from early October 2026 touching trust, provenance, and conflict detection). A follow-up search two days later found still more of the same shape — AXIS Agent Trust Infrastructure's identity/reputation scoring, PiQrypt's hash-chained audit trail, ClawMoat's roadmap-only RFC — plus two 2026 arXiv papers, one proposing a protocol-agnostic trust layer and another concluding flatly that no single trust mechanism suffices for open multi-agent systems. Five or more independent teams quietly building slices of the same thing, none cross-referencing each other, none combining signed proof with a trust score an orchestrator can actually gate decisions on. That's a crowded-but-unsolved gap, not an empty one — and it's worth sitting with that tension rather than pretending the field is clear.

## The Project

Claim Ledger is a drop-in middleware library that sits at the message bus between agents and does three things no single existing project does together: it requires the claiming agent to emit a signed attestation (Ed25519) referencing whatever evidence backs the claim — a tool-call result, an artifact hash, or nothing at all if the claim is pure judgment; it appends every attestation to an append-only, hash-chained ledger so a disputed claim can be replayed and audited after the fact; and it maintains a per-agent, per-claim-type trust score that moves whenever a claim is later confirmed or contradicted — by a ground-truth check, a human reviewer, or a downstream failure. The payoff is a policy hook: a receiving agent's orchestrator can require corroboration or a human sign-off once a peer's trust score for a given claim type drops below a threshold, turning "trust by default" into "trust by evidence and track record."

Concretely, the stack is an Ed25519 claim-signing SDK (Python/TS), a hash-chained attestation ledger queryable by claim ID, agent, or time, a trust-scoring service with configurable decay and claim-type weighting, a policy hook for gating high-stakes actions, and one reference adapter for A2A protocol messages — chosen first because A2A is the protocol most explicitly scoped to cross-organization agent traffic, exactly where "can I trust what this other agent just told me" stops being academic.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: Teams operating multi-agent systems that cross organizational or vendor trust boundaries — agent marketplaces, cross-org A2A pipelines, and agent-to-agent commerce builders (the same cohort building RailBridge/WorkLedger/ReceiptChain-style agent-labor and micropayment infrastructure already in this index) — anywhere a receiving agent cannot inspect the claiming agent's internals and must decide whether to act on its word.
**Mechanism**: Today a false or exaggerated claim from one agent propagates invisibly into whatever the receiving agent does next (approves a payment, skips a redundant check, escalates or doesn't). A signed, ledgered, trust-scored claim layer makes every claim falsifiable after the fact and lets policy gate on a peer's actual track record instead of blind trust, catching a degrading or malicious peer before its claims keep being acted on.
**Measure**: (1) In a pilot multi-agent pipeline, the rate of downstream incidents traceable to an unverified false claim drops measurably after adoption vs. a logged baseline period. (2) Trust-score calibration: for claim types with a ground-truth check available, the trust score predicts claim correctness with a measured AUC/Brier score, not just a vibe. (3) Mean time to detect a consistently-false-claiming peer agent, from "undetected until a downstream failure" today to a bounded number of claims/hours under the scoring policy.

## The Honest Risk

This gap could get absorbed before anyone outside a framework team finishes building for it: A2A or one of the major orchestration frameworks shipping a native attestation primitive is the single biggest threat, and the fact that the HN post framing this as unsolved is barely a week old by the time a second independent search turned up even more narrow attempts (AXIS, PiQrypt, ClawMoat, two arXiv papers) means a better-resourced team could plausibly answer it first. There's also a structural limit worth naming up front: claims that are subjective judgment calls rather than tool-call outputs have no ground truth to score against, so trust scores on those claim types are reputation-only and gameable by an agent that behaves well on checkable claims while lying on the unverifiable ones. And with current engagement sitting at 4 points on the originating post, demand here is still more "a real pain point surfacing independently in five-plus places" than "proven pull" — adoption risk stays real until one of these projects actually wins.
