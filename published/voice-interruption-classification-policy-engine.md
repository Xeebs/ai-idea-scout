You're on hold with your insurance company's voice bot, half-listening as it reads back your benefits, when you say "mm-hm, go on." The bot stops dead, assumes you interrupted it, and asks you to repeat your question. Or the reverse happens: you say "no, I meant Tuesday" and it just keeps talking over you, oblivious. PolyAI's engineering team has put a number on how often this happens — roughly 1 in 5 calls involve a barge-in — and reports that mishandling it measurably drags down call-quality scores. The industry's answer to this, almost universally, is a single on/off switch.

## The Gap

A practitioner runbook from hamming.ai makes the case that barge-in shouldn't be a binary toggle at all — it should be a classification policy. A caller talking over the agent might be issuing a true correction ("no, I meant Tuesday"), a backchannel acknowledgment ("mm-hm, go on"), background noise, a DTMF keypress, a silence timeout, or — in the highest-stakes case — a safety escalation. Each of those six situations calls for a different response, and collapsing them into "stop" or "don't stop" means getting most of them wrong most of the time.

The closest thing to an existing fix is a Hugging Face model called "bargein-classifier" (bnovikov), which does distinguish interruption from backchannel. But its own documentation is candid about its limits: English-only, trained on meeting audio rather than voice-agent call audio, weakly supervised, and recommended for shadow-mode deployment only — not for making real decisions in a live call. Nobody has taken the fuller six-way split past that narrow, explicitly-not-production-ready starting point. Meanwhile current vendor documentation — Smallest AI's among them — still ships only the same binary interruptibility toggle the hamming.ai runbook argues against. The gap isn't a missing idea; it's a missing implementation that actually works on the right kind of audio.

## The Project

The fix is a real-time inline classifier sitting between the VAD/ASR layer and the agent's dialogue manager. Every time it detects an overlap or a pause, it classifies the event into one of the six categories using partial-transcript content, prosody, and timing features, then emits a structured decision — stop-and-listen, continue-and-acknowledge, ignore-as-noise, route-to-DTMF-handler, treat-as-timeout, or escalate-to-human — that the calling application maps onto its own dialogue logic.

It's a model-plus-rules hybrid by design, not a pure ML play: a small fine-tuned classifier handles the genuinely ambiguous cases (correction vs. backchannel), while deterministic rules handle the cases that don't need a model at all (DTMF tones are DTMF tones; a silence duration past threshold is a timeout). The project also has to produce something that doesn't exist yet at all — a labeled evaluation corpus built from real voice-agent call recordings, not meeting audio, since meeting-audio training is precisely why the existing Hugging Face model underperforms in this domain.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: callers to AI voice agents in high-stakes, repeat-contact settings — telehealth triage lines, insurance/benefits intake, and crisis/non-emergency support lines — where PolyAI's reported 1-in-5 barge-in rate means a meaningful fraction of every single call involves an interruption event, and where Forrester's 2026 figure of voice AI handling roughly 19% of inbound contact-center volume means millions of these interactions already happen weekly. Within that population, older callers, non-native speakers, and people calling from noisy environments are disproportionately affected, since backchannel speech patterns and ambient noise are exactly the signals current binary toggles misclassify.

**Mechanism**: correctly classifying "mm-hm" as backchannel instead of a stop-signal lets the agent keep explaining a benefits form instead of abruptly cutting off mid-sentence; correctly classifying a real correction lets the agent stop immediately instead of plowing through an answer the caller already told it was wrong. Both failure directions currently erode trust and push callers to demand a human, so a reusable classifier directly attacks the call-quality penalty PolyAI has already measured.

**Measure**: call-quality/CSAT score delta on calls with at least one barge-in event, before vs. after deployment; false-stop rate (agent incorrectly halts on backchannel) and false-continue rate (agent incorrectly talks over a real correction) on a held-out labeled corpus; rate of calls escalated to a human after a misclassified interruption.

## Try It

There's no shipped skill yet — this idea is tagged Format: BOTH, but no `skills/voice-interruption-classification-policy-engine/` directory exists in this repo at this stage. The idea file sketches what that companion skill, "interruption policy reviewer," would do once built: take a description of a team's voice-agent use case — industry, caller demographics, risk tolerance for cutting off a correction versus talking over a backchannel — and hand back a recommended per-category policy (bias toward always stopping on ambiguous events on a crisis line; bias toward continuing through backchannel on a sales line) plus a checklist of what to log for later retraining. That's a design-judgment exercise distinct from the runtime project: the skill helps a team decide its policy before the classifier exists at all; the project is the live inline engine that enforces it.

Two things are worth sitting with before anyone builds the full version. Domain-matched labeled training data — real voice-agent calls, not meeting audio — is scarce, and getting enough of it may require synthetic generation or a data-sharing partnership with a vendor; that's the main feasibility risk, not the modeling itself. And safety-escalation detection carries real consequences if it's missed or over-triggered — a caller expressing distress needs a separate, more conservative validation path than the other five categories, not the same confidence threshold tuned for "was that a correction or a backchannel."
