# Co-Maker: An LLM-Guided DIY Assistive-Tech Design Assistant for Disabled Makers

The WHO and UNICEF estimate that 2.5 billion people worldwide need an assistive product of some kind, and roughly 1 billion of them are denied access to one — not always because nothing exists, but because what exists doesn't fit. So they build their own. Right now, on r/AssistiveTechnology, that looks like a disabled person soldering a prototype robot, a teacher and OT iterating on a homemade AAC keyboard after a student's feedback, and a quadriplegic cerebral-palsy user writing up a guide for turning a Mac's built-in webcam head-tracking feature into an input device because nothing off-the-shelf worked for them. One poster put the commercial alternative bluntly: most of it "feels like over-engineered plastic trash," and the world doesn't need another "slightly better spoon." Every one of these people solved their own problem — and then the knowledge mostly died with the thread.

## The Gap

Multiple independent posters in r/AssistiveTechnology this past month described building or hacking their own assistive device or software because commercial options were generic, overpriced, or simply didn't match their specific grip, range of motion, or vision. This isn't one frustrated outlier — it's a pattern of people with genuinely different needs converging on the same workaround: teach yourself just enough fabrication or embedded electronics to make the thing nobody will sell you. A CHI 2026 paper (arXiv:2602.13874, out of the University of Wisconsin–Madison) independently confirms this is a live, unsolved research problem — specifically studying LLM-assisted DIY assistive-tech co-making with blind and low-vision users. Searching further turns up only scattered, single-author GitHub projects — a 3-star Gemini-plus-spatial-audio navigation tool, an ESP32 blog build, a Raspberry Pi/ONNX student project — and no reusable, maintained, or evaluated tool that actually helps someone go from "I have this need" to "here's a safe, buildable spec." The default path today is still solo trial-and-error, every single time, for every single person.

## The Project

Co-Maker is a guided design assistant for disabled people, caregivers, and occupational therapists who have no engineering background but do have a specific, concrete need. The user describes the problem, their relevant physical and sensory constraints — grip strength, range of motion, vision, sensation, any assistive device already in use — and what they actually have on hand to build with, whether that's a 3D printer and a soldering iron or just cardboard, tape, and a smartphone. The assistant asks targeted follow-up questions where something safety-relevant is missing, then produces a concrete spec: a parts and materials list with approximate cost, step-by-step assembly instructions, a 3D-print-ready description or simple embedded code where needed, and — critically — safety caveats tied directly to the constraints that person actually stated, not generic boilerplate.

The part that makes this more than a one-off chatbot session is the companion community library: a searchable record of past need-descriptions, the specs they produced, and whether the thing actually got built and worked. That closes exactly the gap the CHI paper flags as missing — individual co-making sessions that never accumulate into shared knowledge. The second person with a similar need starts from a validated design instead of from zero.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: People with disabilities (and their caregivers/OTs) who build or modify their own assistive devices because commercial options don't fit — the WHO/UNICEF estimate cited in this cycle's digest research puts 2.5 billion people worldwide needing an assistive product, with roughly 1 billion currently denied access to one, a gap driven partly by cost and partly by poor fit to individual needs, which is exactly the population self-selecting into r/AssistiveTechnology's DIY threads.

**Mechanism**: Today, a disabled person or caregiver with an idea but no engineering background either gives up, pays for a generic product that doesn't fit, or spends weeks of trial-and-error research to self-teach enough to build something. A guided assistant collapses that research time into a single structured conversation and a concrete, safety-checked spec, and a shared library means the second person with the same need starts from a validated design instead of from zero.

**Measure**: (1) time from "I have this need" to "I have a buildable spec," self-reported by pilot users, versus their own estimate of how long solo research would have taken; (2) rate at which generated specs are actually built and reported working (via a simple built-it/works-for-me confirmation in the library); (3) number of community-library reuses per design — the signal that knowledge is actually accumulating instead of every maker starting over.

## Try It

A companion Claude Code skill exists at [`skills/diy-assistive-tech-codesign-assistant/`](../skills/diy-assistive-tech-codesign-assistant/). Its one-line description: *"Turns a disabled person's, caregiver's, or OT's description of an unmet need plus their physical/sensory constraints and available tools/budget into a buildable low-complexity assistive-device spec (parts list, assembly steps, condition-specific safety caveats). Use when someone wants to DIY/adapt a grip, mount, or simple switch-input device because commercial options don't fit, and explicitly refuses to touch powered mobility, load-bearing, electrical, or near-body/airway devices."*

To use it: either paste the contents of `skills/diy-assistive-tech-codesign-assistant/README.md` into any AI chat tool (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini) along with your need description, constraints, and available tools/budget — or, if you use Claude Code, drop the whole `skills/diy-assistive-tech-codesign-assistant/` folder into `~/.claude/skills/` so Claude reaches for it automatically when you describe a DIY assistive-device need.

Worth knowing before you use it: the skill is deliberately narrow. It only produces specs for adaptive grips, mounts, holders, and simple low-voltage switch/button inputs — it explicitly refuses powered mobility, anything load-bearing on a person, mains electrical, or anything near the airway, eyes, or skin-breaking points, and it never claims a design has been tested or validated by anyone else. Every spec ends with an explicit disclaimer to have an OT, assistive-tech professional, or qualified maker review it before it gets built. That's a real constraint, not a formality — a bad suggestion to someone with limited sensation or motor control could cause real harm, and that's the single biggest risk this project has to design around from day one rather than discover after the fact. It's also worth checking, before investing further, whether established maker communities like Thingiverse's assistive-tech tag or Makers Making Change already cover the simpler end of this need — the open question isn't whether DIY assistive tech happens, it clearly does, but whether LLM-guided, constraint-aware design help specifically is the missing piece, or just a nice-to-have on top of communities that already work reasonably well for easier cases.
