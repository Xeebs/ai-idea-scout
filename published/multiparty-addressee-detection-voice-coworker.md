# Who's Talking To Me: Addressee Detection for Multi-Party AI Coworker Agents

Your AI coworker can finally talk on calls — which means it can finally talk over the wrong person at the wrong time. The vendors who shipped the first speaking meeting bots have quietly admitted, in their own case studies, that they solved the easy version of the problem and shelved the hard one.

## The Gap

The product category is moving fast: AI "coworker" agents that used to sit silently transcribing a meeting are now joining calls as live, speaking participants. A GitHub issue from `anote-ai/Panacea` (`#362`) names the shift directly — "A coworker that can only listen still needs you on the call. To run calls it has to talk, and latency is the make-or-break engineering problem" — and lays the real-time speech pipeline out as a Phase 2 roadmap item, not something already built.

Several teams have since shipped toward exactly this: MeetStream AI launched on Product Hunt with an agent that joins Google Meet and speaks mid-call, Behalf.ai is running a hackathon-stage build on OpenAI's Realtime API with semantic VAD, Softcery has a public Pipecat+Attendee case study, and VideoSDK ships TurnV2 turn-detection models. That's real engineering effort, not vaporware. But look closely at what each of these actually targets: one-on-one or loosely-moderated conversations. The LiveKit/AssemblyAI case study on Boardy — one of the more candid writeups in the space — says it plainly: one-on-one turn-taking is "largely solved." What isn't solved is the problem that only shows up once a third human joins the call: deciding whether the person who just spoke was talking *to the AI* or to one of the other humans in the room. Avatar and voice latency are still sitting at 1-3+ seconds on top of that. No open implementation or benchmark exists for this specific sub-problem — it isn't just an absence from the conversation, it's a gap a shipped product's own documentation names and leaves open.

## The Project

The idea is a real-time addressee-detection module, built as a plugin for the voice-agent stacks teams are already running (Pipecat, LiveKit Agents) rather than a new meeting-bot product to compete with the ones above. On a multi-party call it combines three signals: speaker diarization to know who's talking, lightweight linguistic cues to catch direct address ("Agent, can you pull up the Q3 numbers," names, imperative phrasing aimed outward versus a remark clearly meant for another human), and conversational context (did the agent just get asked something it hasn't answered yet). Those combine into a confidence score for whether the most recent utterance was addressed to the AI, and the agent's response is gated on crossing that threshold.

Because a phone or video call strips out the gaze and body-language cues humans normally rely on for addressee detection in person, the design deliberately doesn't try to brute-force the acoustic signal alone. It leans on the linguistic/context features and keeps a cheap, explicit fallback — a short wake-phrase or name-check — for the genuinely ambiguous cases, instead of overpromising a fully "natural" solution the underlying research doesn't yet support. The components: an off-the-shelf diarization front end, an addressee classifier trained on labeled multi-party meeting transcripts, a confidence-gated response policy (speak only above threshold, otherwise stay silent and keep listening), and — notably — the first reusable evaluation benchmark for this specific sub-problem, built from labeled multi-party call recordings.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: small and mid-size teams (sales, customer success, recruiting, consulting) who adopt AI coworker agents on multi-person calls — a fast-growing segment given that AI meeting-bot products (Otter, Fireflies, and the newer speaking-agent entrants named above) are already standard tooling in these functions; within that population, the direct beneficiaries are the human participants on those calls, who currently experience the bot either staying silent when it should answer or, worse, interrupting a human-to-human exchange because it misreads who's being addressed.

**Mechanism**: accurate addressee detection lets the agent participate only when actually spoken to, which removes the two failure modes that currently make speaking meeting-bots unusable in group settings — unwanted interruptions of human cross-talk, and missed responses when a human does address the bot but phrasing is ambiguous — directly determining whether teams keep the speaking feature enabled or revert to listen-only mode.

**Measure**: addressee-classification precision/recall on a held-out labeled multi-party call corpus; rate of "unwanted interruption" events (agent speaks when a human was addressing another human) per call hour in a pilot deployment; rate of "missed address" events (a human asks the agent something and gets no response) per call hour, with a target of both staying low enough that pilot teams keep the speaking mode enabled after a trial period rather than disabling it.

## Try It

There's no companion skill for this one — the gap calls for a runtime module living inside a live audio pipeline (diarization, streaming classification, response gating), not a prompt-driven task an AI chat assistant can execute on demand. The MVP path is a Pipecat plugin plus a labeled evaluation corpus: build the diarization + linguistic-cue classifier, run it against 3-4-person call recordings with addressee annotations, and pilot it with one real team for two weeks, tracking unwanted-interruption and missed-address rates against a naive "respond to anything that sounds like a request" baseline.

Worth saying plainly: this is the hardest kind of gap to close. The source case study that surfaced it calls multi-party turn-taking an open, unsolved problem in its own right, so a first build should be scored as "measurably better than the naive baseline," not "solved." Labeled multi-party-call-with-addressee data is scarce, which may force synthetic generation or a data-sharing partnership. And overlapping speech degrades diarization quality independently of addressee detection — on noisy calls, the accuracy ceiling may be set by the diarization step, not the classifier sitting on top of it. The wake-phrase fallback for truly ambiguous utterances may end up doing more of the real work than the "natural" detection layer the pitch leads with.
