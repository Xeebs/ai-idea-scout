# Graph Diet: An Entity-Resolution and Token-Cost Governor for Production GraphRAG

GraphRAG was supposed to be the upgrade: swap flat vector retrieval for a knowledge graph and get multi-hop reasoning that actually understands how your documents relate to each other. A lot of teams tried it. A smaller number of them are now quietly ripping it back out — not because the idea was wrong, but because nobody shipped the plumbing that keeps it affordable and accurate once the graph stops being a demo.

## The Gap

The complaint shows up in almost identical language across completely unconnected corners of the internet. A developer on r/Rag, writing up "My experience with GraphRAG," concludes flatly that the technique is "not mature enough" for production — pointing to thin documentation, poor scaling, and graph connections that become unmanageable as the document set grows. Independent analysis from tianpan.co's GraphRAG production series backs this up with a number that's hard to ignore: naive GraphRAG token consumption can run orders of magnitude higher than standard RAG for equivalent retrieval — one source cited puts it as high as ~6,000x — and deployments have a habit of "failing quietly" after an initially promising pilot. A LlamaIndex community thread adds a third independent voice describing GraphRAG as "not yet useful or production-ready."

What makes this gap interesting is what it's *not*. The underlying graph engines are real and actively maintained — microsoft/graphrag, HKUDS/LightRAG, and FalkorDB's dedicated "GraphRAG SDK 1.0" all target production use cases, and neo4j-labs/llm-graph-builder is right there too. The technology exists. Atlan's 2026 guide still rates the overall ecosystem's production maturity as "early-to-mid, with limited enterprise tooling," and a widely-cited Gradient Flow assessment finds that production deployments delivering real business value are rare — "most implementations remain experimental." So this isn't a story about a missing graph database. It's a story about teams hitting two specific, named walls — entity resolution falling apart as the graph grows, and query cost going unbounded — with no existing layer built specifically to catch either one.

## The Project

Graph Diet is a governing layer that sits alongside an existing GraphRAG framework rather than replacing it, because replacing a maintained graph engine was never the problem that needed solving. It wraps microsoft/graphrag and LightRAG specifically — the two most-adopted targets found in a `gh search repos "GraphRAG"` scan — and attacks the two failure points named in the evidence directly.

The first piece is incremental, confidence-scored entity resolution: instead of re-resolving the entire graph every time new documents land (the thing that turns "graph connections become unmanageable" from a vague complaint into a literal compute problem), it batches and dedupes entities as they're added, carrying a confidence score so downstream queries know how much to trust a given merge. The second piece is a token-budget governor sitting in front of query execution — it caps multi-hop traversal depth and breadth dynamically against a per-query cost ceiling, and when a query would blow that budget, it falls back to standard vector retrieval instead of silently running the expensive query anyway. That fallback is the whole point: GraphRAG's current failure mode is invisible until the bill or the latency shows up. Graph Diet's job is to make the tradeoff a visible, logged decision instead of a silent cliff.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: Engineering teams who have already adopted or piloted GraphRAG (microsoft/graphrag, LightRAG, or similar) for multi-hop reasoning — enterprise-search, research-knowledge-graph, and compliance teams — and are the ones hitting the exact scaling wall described in the r/Rag thread and corroborating blogs after an initially promising pilot.
**Mechanism**: Capping traversal cost and incrementally resolving entities instead of re-processing the whole graph turns GraphRAG from something that "fails quietly" in production (per tianpan.co) into something with a predictable cost ceiling and a known entity-resolution error rate, letting teams keep the multi-hop reasoning benefit without the unbounded cost/maintenance blowup that caused the r/Rag author to abandon it.
**Measure**: token cost per query before vs. after the governor, targeting a bounded multiplier over standard RAG rather than the reported ~6,000x; entity-resolution precision/recall on a graph that has grown incrementally vs. one fully re-resolved from scratch; rate of queries falling back to vector retrieval under the cost ceiling (a visible, logged tradeoff instead of a silent quality cliff).

## Try It

There's no packaged skill or runnable prototype for this one yet — it's still at the idea stage, which is exactly why it's worth publishing now rather than later. If you're already running GraphRAG in production and recognize either failure mode described above, that's the signal this writeup exists to surface. The project's own MVP sketch calls for building the cost governor first (a traversal-depth/breadth cap with vector fallback, wrapped around microsoft/graphrag) and benchmarking it against a corpus sized to reproduce the reported cost blowup, before layering in the incremental entity-resolution batch step — a 2-4 week scope for one or two people.

Worth noting before you build: the idea file flags its own strongest counter-evidence directly — FalkorDB already ships a dedicated production GraphRAG SDK, yet the r/Rag and Gradient Flow sources still describe the broader ecosystem as largely experimental even with that SDK available. That's worth testing against these two specific failure modes before assuming a new governing layer is actually needed on top of what FalkorDB already offers. It's also worth being honest that incremental entity resolution is a hard problem in its own right — coreference drift as a graph grows isn't something a short build cycle fully solves, and a hard cost ceiling with vector fallback will change answer quality on complex multi-hop queries in ways that need measuring, not assuming.
