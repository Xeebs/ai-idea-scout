A client paid a freelance developer to rip an AI agent out of the exact tool that developer built them. Not because the agent was dumb — because it never knew when to stop and ask. That story picked up 528 points and 110 comments on r/AI_Agents, and it landed in the same 30-day window as a quieter, more pointed question from the same community: "Should AI prompt human more?" Eleven points isn't a viral number, but 33 comments on a yes/no question is — it was the most argued-over on-topic thread in the whole corpus. Everyone agrees agents should interrupt a human at the right moment. Nobody has a mechanism for knowing what that moment is.

## The Gap

The tooling to let an agent *ask* a human already exists and is mature: HumanLayer sits at nearly 11,000 GitHub stars and was actively maintained as of the research window, and LangGraph ships interrupt primitives out of the box. 2026's crop of human-in-the-loop guides even catalog the usual triggers — confidence thresholds, hard-coded policy topics, sentiment or repetition detection. But those same guides include an admission that undercuts all of it: confidence scores lie. Every one of those trigger policies is hand-rolled, bespoke to whatever deployment it's bolted onto, re-derived from scratch by whoever builds it. A GitHub search for "agent escalation policy" turned up zero repositories. The plumbing for *how* to ask is solved. The judgment layer for *when* to ask is not — and the 528-point thread is what it costs to get that judgment wrong: an agent that acted instead of asking, and a client relationship that didn't survive the mistake.

## The Project

The core idea is to treat "should this action run on its own, or should it stop and ask first" as a scored decision, not a vibe. An escalation-policy library sits between an agent's proposed action and its execution, and scores that action on three explicit axes: ambiguity (does the instruction actually cover this, or is the agent improvising?), reversibility (can this be undone, cheaply, in a reasonable window?), and blast radius (who or what gets hurt if this goes wrong?). A declarative policy file turns that score into one of three outcomes — proceed, ask, or refuse.

The design choice that matters most is keeping the decision layer separate from the delivery plumbing. This isn't a competitor to HumanLayer or LangGraph interrupts — it's the missing piece that decides *whether* to fire them. The decision emits a standard escalation event; HumanLayer, LangGraph, or a Slack webhook can pick it up and deliver it however a given stack already works. And because "confidence scores lie" is the whole reason this gap exists, the project ships with a labeled escalation benchmark — real agent actions, hand-labeled by humans as should-have-escalated or shouldn't-have — so a policy's precision and recall are a measured number, not a claim.

Components: a cheap action-scoring layer combining deterministic irreversibility rules with lightweight ambiguity classification; a declarative policy format; adapters for HumanLayer and LangGraph; and the labeled benchmark plus eval harness that makes any of this falsifiable.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: Freelance developers and small agencies building agent automations for SMB clients — the cohort of the 528-point rip-the-AI-out thread, where one botched autonomy boundary cost the entire engagement. Upwork and Fiverr list thousands of active "AI agent developer" freelancers, and r/AI_Agents (the thread's home) counts hundreds of thousands of members; the directly affected group is plausibly the tens of thousands of builders shipping agents to non-technical clients without any principled escalation layer.

**Mechanism**: Today these builders either over-restrict (agent asks constantly, client sees no value) or over-trust (agent acts wrongly without asking, client rips it out); a measured policy layer lets them set the boundary deliberately and show clients the receipts ("the agent escalates irreversible actions; here's its precision on the benchmark"), converting trust from a leap of faith into a configured, demonstrable property — fewer torn-out deployments, fewer interruption-fatigued clients.

**Measure**: (1) Escalation precision/recall on the labeled benchmark ≥0.85/0.85, vs. measured baselines for naive confidence thresholds. (2) In pilot deployments, wrongful autonomous actions (actions a human later reverses or complains about) drop ≥50%. (3) Interruption rate stays under a client-set budget (e.g., <5 asks/day) while holding recall on irreversible actions — proving it's not just asking about everything.

## Try It

A companion skill already exists at `skills/agent-escalation-policy-engine/` — built as the seed example for this pipeline's skill track. Its one-line description: "Scores one proposed AI-agent action on ambiguity, reversibility, and blast radius, and recommends PROCEED, ASK, or REFUSE with justification."

It's the hand-scoring version of the same three-axis judgment the runtime library would eventually automate — useful right now, before any library exists, for anyone reviewing an agent's action log after the fact or sketching a policy file before shipping one. To use it: paste the `README.md` from that folder into any AI chat tool along with the action you want reviewed and the original instruction the agent was given, or drop the whole `skills/agent-escalation-policy-engine/` folder into `~/.claude/skills/` if you're working in Claude Code. Either way, it hands back PROCEED / ASK / REFUSE with a justification tied to the actual facts you gave it — and it's built to say "ASK" rather than guess whenever a fact it needs is missing.

Worth sitting with before building the runtime version: this could already be absorbed. LangChain or HumanLayer are the obvious candidates to ship their own opinionated escalation-policy layer on top of their existing interrupt plumbing, and if either does, a standalone project here gets undercut fast — that's the strongest already-solved risk on the table. There's also a harder-to-dodge problem baked into the benchmark itself: "should have escalated" labels may just encode one annotator's risk tolerance, not a standard that generalizes across domains. And the ambiguity axis leans on an LLM judging an LLM's intent — the exact "confidence scores lie" trap this whole idea exists to route around — which is why the deterministic reversibility and blast-radius axes have to carry most of the real weight, not the judgment call.
