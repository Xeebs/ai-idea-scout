# TrustedTriage: A Validated, Maintained On-Device Triage Assistant for Community Health Workers

Half a dozen developers have independently built the same offline AI triage tool for community health workers in the last month. None of them has let a clinician check its work.

## The Gap

A dev.to field report titled "Building medical AI for the other 90%" and a companion HLTH industry piece both name the same structural failure in plain language: every triage tool built for a community health worker (CHW) has to run fully offline — on a phone CPU or a district-clinic laptop that might see hours of connectivity a month — and yet, as the HLTH piece puts it, "there is no requirement or incentive for developers to test AI tools in rural settings, include rural data in training sets, or design for low-bandwidth environments." That's not a hypothetical gap. The research digest surfaced a real cluster of people already building toward exactly this: Trij (an open-source offline PWA for CHW triage), Sentinel Health (a Gemma-based offline tool with its own deterministic safety layer, built for the Gemma 4 Good Hackathon), PocketTriage, TriageOS, SHIFA, TriageMate, and Google's MedGemma sitting one layer up as a general-purpose model rather than a full triage app.

So the technique isn't missing — six-plus teams converged on "small on-device model plus offline-first design" independently and without coordinating. What's missing is everything after the prototype: not one of those builds has published a clinical evaluation protocol, several are explicitly self-labeled "unvalidated prototype v0.1," and none shows signs of being maintained past its hackathon or solo-developer origin. A tool that tells a health worker whether to refer a patient is only as trustworthy as the process that checked it — and right now that process doesn't exist anywhere in this space.

## The Project

The pitch is not a new triage app to add to the pile — it's the one piece none of the existing six combine: a maintained release discipline wrapped around the same core architecture everyone else already landed on. Three things make it different. First, a deterministic safety layer: the model drafts a triage suggestion, but a clinician-authored decision tree gates every output before it reaches the CHW, never the reverse — the model never gets to override the tree. Second, a published, versioned evaluation protocol: sensitivity and specificity measured against a held-out, clinician-labeled symptom set, reviewed by a clinical advisory partner, run before every release rather than once at launch and never again. Third, a packaging and update pipeline built for the actual connectivity reality — SMS or occasional Wi-Fi sync — so a clinic laptop that connects for an hour a month still receives safety-layer patches instead of running on a frozen build indefinitely.

The LLM itself — a quantized Gemma-class model — does the part it's actually good at: symptom elicitation and documentation, turning a conversation into structured data. Every decision that gates a referral runs through the deterministic tree instead. It's a narrower claim than "AI triages patients," and that narrowness is the point: the gap report is explicit that the under-built piece isn't the model, it's the governance and maintenance around it.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: Community health workers serving the roughly 1.4 billion people the WHO and ITU estimate live in areas with persistently low or no internet connectivity for health services — concentrated in rural sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and remote Latin America. Within that population, an estimated 2-4 million CHWs globally are running triage decisions off manual paper protocols today, with no digital second opinion at all.

**Mechanism**: Without connectivity, a CHW facing an ambiguous case today has three options: guess, call a supervisor who may be hours away, or default to a blanket policy — over-referring (costly transport, overloaded clinics) or under-referring (missed emergencies). A validated on-device tool with an auditable hard safety gate gives that worker a structured second opinion at the point of contact, with referral thresholds a clinician set and can later amend, instead of a black box or a coin flip.

**Measure**: (1) sensitivity and specificity of the tool's referral recommendations against a clinician-labeled validation set, published with every release — not just at launch; (2) change in inappropriate-referral rate versus a paper-protocol control arm during a pilot-district rollout; (3) number of independent clinic deployments still active six months after install, which is the specific metric every prototype in this cluster has so far failed to report at all.

## Try It

There's no companion skill here — this is runtime medical-decision infrastructure that has to run on a CHW's actual device under real connectivity constraints, not a prompt-driven task a chat assistant executes on demand. The honest MVP is narrower than "a triage app": pick one well-bounded domain where a WHO IMCI-style decision tree already exists as a deterministic backbone (wound/skin-lesion or respiratory-symptom triage are good starting points), run a quantized Gemma 2B/4B via llama.cpp or MediaPipe on Android against 100-200 clinician-labeled historical cases, and report sensitivity/specificity before touching a real deployment. If it can't clear a clinically-set bar on retrospective data, the project stops there — it does not go near a live clinic.

Worth being honest about the risks the idea file itself flags. Regulatory exposure varies by country and almost certainly requires an early ministry-of-health pilot partnership, not something to bolt on after the fact. Liability for a wrong referral decision needs an explicit "triage aid, not diagnosis" scope boundary built into the UX, not buried in a terms-of-service page nobody reads. There's a real chance this duplicates an NGO's unpublished in-house tool — a direct outreach check to Dimagi, PATH, and Living Goods before building is cheap insurance against that. And the hardest, slowest part of the whole MVP is securing a real clinical advisor and labeled validation data — if that partnership doesn't materialize, no amount of engineering rescues the project.
