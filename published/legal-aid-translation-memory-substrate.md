# Legal Lexicon: A Shared, Open Translation-Memory Substrate for Multilingual Legal-Aid Assistants

Right now, somewhere, a legal-aid chatbot is quietly mistranslating the word "garnishment" for a client who doesn't speak English — and the organization closest to fixing that, Stanford's own Legal Design Lab, has said in public that the shared fix doesn't exist yet. It's still "gathering glossaries that already exist." Meanwhile at least two independent legal-aid chatbots are already live and each building their own translation layer from scratch, in parallel, with no shared ground to stand on.

## The Gap

Multiple independent multilingual legal-aid chatbots already exist — LawGlance, a separate open-source "ai-legal-assistant" project, plus a cluster of regional tools like Lone Star Legal Aid's Navi, NC Legal Aid's LIA, and Hong Kong's PoBot. Each one, presumably, is building its own ad hoc translation and legal-terminology handling, because there is nowhere shared to pull from. The people best positioned to know this is a problem — Stanford's Legal Design Lab — proposed exactly the fix the field needs: an open, shared translation-memory database for legal help. But their own public writeup is explicit that the project has only "started gathering glossaries that already exist." That's a rare thing in this kind of search: not a guess about what's missing, but a written admission, from the domain expert closest to the problem, that the shared substrate isn't built. A broader search for anything adjacent turns up only tangential prior art — the EU's DGT-TM/IATE system, which is scoped to EU statutory law, not legal aid, and a 2012 Japanese law-translation-memory paper that's old and generically legal rather than aid-specific. Nothing found is built for the actual use case: a legal-aid client trying to understand "eviction," "ex parte," or "garnishment" in their own language, where a generic machine-translation system routinely flattens precise legal meaning into something misleading or wrong.

## The Project

The idea is an open, community-maintained translation-memory and legal-terminology-glossary database built specifically for legal-aid contexts, not general or commercial legal translation. Every entry is a term-pair tagged with jurisdiction, practice area — housing, family, immigration, benefits — and a disambiguating example sentence, because legal terms carry precise meanings that shift by context and that a flat dictionary lookup or a generic MT system will miss. Instead of staying a static glossary, the database is exposed as a queryable API/MCP connector that any legal-aid chatbot can call directly, getting back a vetted term and its example usage rather than a bare word-for-word guess. None of this works without trust, so a moderation workflow — legal-aid staff and interpreters reviewing and approving submissions before they ever go live — sits in front of anything served to a real client. That's the layer the Legal Design Lab's own glossary-gathering effort hadn't reached yet, and it's the layer that turns a pile of collected glossaries into something other software can actually depend on.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: The estimated 26 million U.S. residents with limited English proficiency (Census ACS data) who seek legal-aid help in a language other than English, and the independent legal-aid chatbot projects (LawGlance, Navi, LIA, PoBot, and any future entrant) currently each building their own translation/terminology layer from scratch with no shared substrate to draw from.
**Mechanism**: Today a legal-aid assistant either skips multilingual support, relies on generic MT that mistranslates precise legal terms, or hand-builds its own glossary in isolation — work the Legal Design Lab itself confirms nobody has shared yet. A queryable shared substrate lets any adopting tool return a vetted, jurisdiction-aware translation for a legal term instead of a generic MT guess, and lets a new multilingual legal-aid tool launch without first re-deriving a glossary other teams already built.
**Measure**: (1) number of distinct legal-aid tools/orgs querying the substrate in production after 6 months (adoption, the direct test of whether "shared substrate" demand is real); (2) term-translation accuracy on a held-out set of legal-aid-specific terms, benchmarked against generic MT on the same set; (3) glossary coverage growth (number of validated term-pairs x language-pair x practice-area combinations) over time, as the Legal Design Lab's own "started gathering" effort is the current baseline to beat.

## Risks worth naming up front

This isn't a clean slate. The strongest reason this might already be solved — or might be redundant to build — is that the Legal Design Lab may simply be further along internally than its public writeup suggests; anyone picking this up should check directly with them before duplicating effort. There's also a real liability dimension that doesn't get hand-waved away by good intentions: a wrong translated legal term in a client-facing context has consequences, so the moderation and verification gate has to be a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have, before any term goes live. And getting existing chatbot teams to actually swap their own working (if duplicated) translation code for a new shared API is as much an adoption and trust problem as a technical one — this needs a committed pilot partner before a full build, not just a hopeful API spec. Finally, the entire value proposition rests on the jurisdiction/practice-area tagging being fine-grained enough to actually disambiguate real terms; if it's too coarse, the project risks quietly becoming generic MT with extra metadata bolted on.
