# VerifyAid: A Structured Verification Workflow for Non-Expert Legal-Aid Reviewers

Every legal-aid chatbot project turns up the same quiet confession once you look past the launch announcement. Lone Star Legal Aid built "Navi." North Carolina Legal Aid built "LIA." Linklaters' pro bono attorneys in Hong Kong built "PoBot" for the labor-rights group Migrasia. Each one drafts intake summaries, eligibility assessments, or forms for people who can't afford a lawyer — and in each case, a human still has to read every line before anyone relies on it. A Linklaters pro bono partner put it plainly: much of the project's time ends up "dedicated to verifying the AI's findings." The chatbots got built. The verification step didn't. It's still a person, line by line, doing the thing the AI was supposed to make faster.

## The Gap

Across Navi, LIA, and PoBot — three independently built tools, funded and staffed by different organizations — the pattern is identical: generate first, manually re-check everything after. That manual re-check isn't a minor friction cost; it's the actual ceiling on how many cases an AI-assisted legal-aid program can handle, because it consumes the exact scarce resource (attorney and pro bono hours) the chatbot was meant to free up. The Legal Services Corporation, which funds both Navi and LIA through Technology Initiative Grants, estimates that roughly 92% of low-income Americans' civil legal needs go unmet or under-met each year. A verification bottleneck that caps case throughput isn't a side issue to that number — it's a direct constraint on it.

Meanwhile, the verification *technology* itself isn't missing from the market. Mature citation-checking products — Clearbrief, CiteCheck AI, BriefCatch RealityCheck, CiteSentinel, Beyond Assured/Benchly — already do structured, automated AI-output verification. Every single one of them, though, is built and priced for law firms checking case-law citations in litigation documents. None is built for the very different reviewer sitting on the other side of a legal-aid intake form: a non-lawyer or junior pro bono volunteer checking a plain-language eligibility summary against a benefits program's rules, not checking a brief against case law. That's a narrower, cheaper, and — per every example in the digest — currently unaddressed slice of the same underlying problem.

## The Project

VerifyAid is built for that specific reviewer, not the law-firm one. Feed it the AI-drafted intake summary or form alongside the source material it's supposed to be grounded in — a benefits program's eligibility rules, a visa category's requirements — and it pulls out each individual factual or eligibility claim in the draft, hunts for the exact passage in the source that should back it up, and lays the two side by side. A claim with a matching passage gets a quick green light the reviewer can scan past in seconds. A claim with no match found gets flagged "unverified — needs attorney review," explicitly, instead of sliding through un-noticed the way it would in an unstructured read-through.

The scope is deliberately narrow: intake, eligibility, and form-drafting verification, not case-law citation-checking. That's the commoditized half of this problem — the half Clearbrief and friends already own. VerifyAid is built for the half nobody packaged: the reviewer who isn't a litigator, checking output that isn't a brief.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: Legal-aid nonprofits and their pro bono attorney networks serving low-income clients in immigration, public-benefits, and domestic-worker cases — the Legal Services Corporation (LSC), which funded both Navi and LIA via Technology Initiative Grants, estimates roughly 92% of low-income Americans' civil legal needs go unmet or under-met annually, and the specific bottleneck this targets is the attorney-hours constraint limiting how many of those cases an AI-assisted intake program can actually process.
**Mechanism**: Today, every AI-drafted intake output requires a full manual attorney re-check because there's no structured way to see which claims are grounded and which aren't — this consumes the same scarce attorney hours the AI tool was meant to free up. A side-by-side claim-to-source verification view lets a reviewer confirm grounded claims in seconds and focus full attention only on the flagged unverified ones, cutting the review time per case.
**Measure**: (1) median minutes per case spent on manual verification before vs. after adoption, measured with a partner legal-aid org; (2) percentage of AI-drafted claims the tool correctly flags as unverified against a human-attorney-labeled gold set (false-negative rate on missed errors is the critical metric — a tool that misses an ungrounded claim is worse than no tool); (3) number of additional cases a given attorney-hour budget can process per month post-adoption.

## Try It

This idea is tagged `Format: BOTH`, but no companion skill has been built yet — there's no `skills/legal-aid-ai-verification-workflow/` on disk at the time of this writeup. The intended skill version (from the idea file's Skill Angle) would take a single case at a time: hand it an AI-drafted intake summary plus the source eligibility rules it should be grounded in, and it returns a claim-by-claim report — supported, unsupported, or partially supported, each with the matched source passage or an explicit "no match found." That's the one-shot check a reviewer runs per case; the full project is the persistent tool that stores source documents, keeps a verification history across cases for audit purposes, and plugs into whatever chatbot platform an org already runs.

Until that skill exists, if you're a legal-aid org wanting to test the underlying idea now, the move described in the idea's MVP sketch is to bound the scope to one domain with enumerable rules (one state's public-benefits eligibility criteria, say) and run the claim-extraction-and-source-matching pipeline against past drafts with known attorney corrections — then check how often it actually catches what the attorney had to fix.

Worth saying plainly, because it's the strongest reason this might not be worth building: before doing any of that, try pointing a general commercial citation-checker like Clearbrief or CiteCheck AI at a real legal-aid intake draft first. If one of them can be configured or prompted to cover this reviewer's job well enough, a dedicated tool may be solving a problem that's already one prompt away from solved. And because legal-aid orgs are liability-sensitive, any tool that claims to "verify" legal content needs a hard scope disclaimer and probably legal review of the tool itself — a false sense of security from an imperfect verifier is worse than today's fully-manual process, which is exactly why the false-negative rate on missed errors has to be the primary gate before any real deployment, not an afterthought metric.
