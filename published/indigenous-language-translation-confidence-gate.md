# No Entiendo, Flagged: A Confidence and Escalation Gate for Indigenous-Language AI Translation

Type "no entiendo" into a chatbot in Spanish and something is clearly broken. Type it in Mixtec or Zapotec, and the AI translation layer behind that chatbot may just... repeat the phrase back, or loop on the same unhelpful terms — with the same confident UI chrome, the same unbroken-looking response, as if nothing is wrong. The person on the other end has no way to tell the difference between "this translated correctly" and "this silently failed." For a parent trying to understand a school letter about their child, or a patient trying to follow health instructions, that invisible failure is the whole problem.

## The Gap

In Greenfield, California — the Salinas Valley farmworker community EdSource reported on in March 2026 — Triqui- and Mixteco-speaking parents say they can't advocate for their children without an interpreter. The school district leans partly on Nurbli, an AI-based over-the-phone interpretation app, while state data shows a severe undercount of the students who actually speak these languages (7,681 Mixteco speakers and 374 Zapotec speakers statewide in 2024-25, per California's own figures). The Middlebury Institute has separately documented that more than 20,000 Salinas Valley immigrants speak neither English nor Spanish, and that even the specialized human-interpreter services built for this community — Indigenous Interpreting+ — openly struggle to cover the sheer number of language variants in play.

Then, in September 2026, a Mexican report from grupoanimal.mx tested chatbots directly with Zapotec and Mixtec speakers and found a specific, recurring failure mode: the bot would repeat the same terms back, or just say "no entiendo" — a breakdown the speakers said flatly wouldn't happen if they'd asked in Spanish. That's not a hunch; it's a documented, reproducible pattern.

It would be easy to assume this is just a coverage gap waiting on a bigger model. It isn't, entirely. Google Translate added Zapotec, Mixtec, and Nahuatl support in 2025, and ModelFront advertises "full support" for both Mixtec and Zapotec — these are shipped, maintained products, not research demos. But academic machine-translation literature on Mixtec-Spanish shows wildly inconsistent quality depending on the domain: BLEU scores around 17-22 on general text, versus 96-99 on a narrow educational or religious-text corpus. Layer on INALI's count of 81 distinct Mixtec variants and 50+ Zapotec languages, often differing town to town, and you get exactly the mismatch that produces grupoanimal.mx's "no entiendo" failures — a general-purpose model doing fine on the training distribution it was tuned for and quietly falling apart on the specific variant spoken in a specific school district. A search of GitHub for "Mixtec Zapotec translation" or "indigenous language machine translation low resource" turns up nothing. People are discussing this failure in the press and in advocacy reporting. Nobody appears to have built the thing that would catch it before it reaches the family relying on it.

## The Project

The fix here isn't a better translation model — it's a gate in front of the ones that already exist. The idea is a gateway that sits between a caller and existing MT APIs (Google Translate, ModelFront), inspects every output against known failure signatures — repeated-term loops, literal "no entiendo" responses, low API-reported confidence, mismatches against a community-sourced glossary of common school and health phrases — and, when something looks wrong, either substitutes a glossary-corrected answer or flags the exchange for escalation to a human interpreter (through a service like Indigenous Interpreting+) instead of serving a degraded answer with the same apparent confidence as a correct one.

The centerpiece is the confidence-scoring and escalation logic plus a maintained, per-variant glossary — not a competing translation model. This is deliberate: Google and ModelFront have already done the expensive work of building MT for these languages. What's missing is the layer that makes the quality gap *visible* the moment it happens, instead of leaving a parent or health-visit participant to discover it on their own, the way grupoanimal.mx's interviewees did.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: indigenous-language-speaking farmworkers and their parent/student family members interacting with AI-mediated translation in school and health settings. Same CA state data as above — specifically the Greenfield/Salinas Valley families EdSource cites who say they can't advocate for their children without reliable interpretation, within a national population the National Center for Farmworker Health estimates at several hundred thousand indigenous-language speakers.
**Mechanism**: detects the documented "no entiendo"/repeated-term failure mode before it reaches the parent or health-visit participant, and routes to a community-validated correction or a human interpreter instead of letting a degraded translation masquerade as equally reliable to the Spanish output — closing the specific visibility gap, not just the raw quality gap, that the grupoanimal.mx report's interviewees flagged.
**Measure**: (1) reduction in silent-failure rate ("no entiendo"/repeated-term responses reaching the end user) on a held-out test set of common school/health phrases per target variant; (2) precision/recall of the escalation flag against a hand-labeled set of known-bad translations; (3) parent/advocate-reported trust in translated guidance, collected via a partner school-district pilot (e.g. Greenfield Union School District, the EdSource-cited case).

## Try It

There's no companion `skills/` build for this idea yet, so there's nothing to drop into Claude Code or paste into a chat tool today. The idea file does sketch a skill-shaped angle worth watching for: a one-shot risk-check that takes a phrase or short document plus a target variant and context (school notice, health instruction), and returns the MT translation alongside a confidence flag — and, if flagged, either a glossary-corrected alternative or an explicit "use a human interpreter" recommendation. That would be a lighter, starter-glossary version of the full gateway, useful for a school staffer or community volunteer checking a single message before sending it, distinct from the always-on production gateway described above.

## Worth Knowing Before You Build This

The strongest reason this might not be greenfield: Nurbli or Indigenous Interpreting+ may already run an internal confidence-scoring step that just isn't publicly documented — anyone pursuing this should reach out directly before assuming nothing exists. The dialect fragmentation that makes this problem real also makes it hard to solve generally: a glossary validated for one town's Mixtec variant may not transfer to the next town over, so any real build needs to scope its claims narrowly to the specific variant it's actually tested against. And a confidence gate is a blunt instrument if tuned wrong in either direction — too loose and known failure modes slip through as false negatives; too aggressive and every translation gets escalated, which defeats the purpose and erodes the trust it's meant to protect.
