A VR study of 30 participants found 93.3% of them preferred talking to a voice agent that tracked their emotional state continuously over one that waited to slot their tone into "happy," "sad," or "angry" and respond from a script. That preference gap is wide enough to notice — and wide enough that it's strange nobody has turned it into something you can actually install. Every team that has built the better version has built it once, for one paper, and then moved on.

## The Gap

The default architecture for voice-agent emotion handling is still classify-then-template: bucket the caller's speech into a small fixed taxonomy of basic emotions, then reach for a canned response keyed to whichever bucket won. A CallSphere practitioner blog calls that standard seven-basic-emotions approach "unreliable on phone audio" — which tracks, because phone audio is exactly the noisy, low-fidelity signal a discrete classifier is worst at. The alternative that keeps showing up in research is conditioning the reply and the TTS prosody on a continuous valence-arousal estimate, recomputed every turn instead of locked in at session start. Sympatheia does this with a speech-to-speech system explicitly "conditioned on affect inferred from the user's speech." AffAdapt updates an embodied persona's affect state turn by turn. The 93.3%-preference VR study is testing exactly this continuous, prosody-driven approach against the alternative.

None of it has crossed into something reusable. Every implementation found — Sympatheia, AffAdapt, EmoSteer-TTS (which at least ships runnable code via training-free activation steering), CoCoEmo, a PAD-based TTS framework — is a research artifact scored on its own author-built benchmark, not a component anyone else has picked up and dropped into a production voice stack. A search of GitHub for "affect aware TTS" turned up zero hits; "emotion voice agent" surfaced two near-empty student repos. The one production-facing claim in the space, VoiceGenie.ai's promise that it "adapts tone and dialogue dynamically," is marketing copy with no documented implementation behind it — not evidence the gap is closed. That combination — real, working research code on one side, and nothing adopted or shipped on the other — is what keeps this gap open instead of closed.

## The Project

The idea is a middleware layer, not a new voice-agent product: something that sits inside an existing voice-agent stack and does one job well. Each turn, it infers a continuous valence-arousal estimate from the caller's speech — using open, already-runnable approaches like activation-steering TTS techniques or lightweight prosody feature extraction, rather than training anything from scratch — and feeds that signal into two places at once: the content of the agent's reply (phrasing, pacing, de-escalation cues) and the prosody of the TTS output. The emphasis on *continuous* and *per-turn* is the whole point. A discrete-label system reacts to a snapshot; this tracks a trajectory, which matters most exactly where mismatched tone does the most damage — low-acuity but emotionally loaded calls, not generic small talk.

Four pieces make up the system: a per-turn affect estimator, a response-conditioning adapter that nudges an LLM's reply style based on the current affect signal, a TTS prosody adapter, and an evaluation harness that scores the whole pipeline against a discrete-taxonomy baseline on a labeled call set. None of it requires proprietary training infrastructure — EmoSteer-TTS's activation-steering approach and comparable LoRA-based conditioning methods are runnable on open models today, which is itself new enough to be worth calling out: this kind of middleware wasn't buildable on open tooling a year ago.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: callers to non-clinical check-in and support voice lines that already use AI — e.g., employee-assistance-program wellness check-ins, post-discharge follow-up calls, and benefits/claims support lines where people are reluctant to state distress directly and a mismatched chipper tone increases call abandonment.
**Mechanism**: a continuous affect signal lets the agent detect rising distress or frustration from prosody even when the words themselves are neutral, and adjust tone and pacing (or trigger escalation to a human) before the caller disengages — the gap the discrete-taxonomy approach misses because it only reacts to labeled categories, not a changing trajectory.
**Measure**: caller-reported rapport/comfort score vs. discrete-taxonomy baseline; call abandonment rate; escalation-accuracy (does the system flag genuine distress trajectories that a human reviewer agrees warranted escalation) on a held-out labeled call set.
