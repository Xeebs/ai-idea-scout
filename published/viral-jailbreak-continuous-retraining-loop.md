# Fresh Fence: A Continuous Jailbreak-Feed Retraining Loop for Guardrail Classifiers

A working jailbreak doesn't stay a secret. Someone posts it on Reddit, it gets refined in the replies, and within hours it's circulating as a known, reusable bypass — faster than almost any production team's retraining cycle can react. Guardrail classifiers, meanwhile, are shipped the way most ML models are shipped: trained on a snapshot, frozen, and refreshed on a schedule measured in weeks or quarters. The result is a standing window where a publicly known jailbreak technique is already loose on the internet while the classifier defending your product has no idea it exists.

## The Gap

An industry guide cited during research for this idea makes the claim plainly: "users share working jailbreaks on Reddit within hours of discovery." That's the whole problem in one sentence — the attack-sharing loop runs on a cadence of hours, and the defense-updating loop, for almost everyone running a guardrail classifier in production, runs on a cadence of weeks or longer. Nothing found in the research wires a continuously-refreshed jailbreak feed directly into a classifier's live decision boundary at that speed. The closest things that exist are adjacent, not matching: the academic "JailbreaksOverTime" paper (arXiv 2504.19440) shows that weekly self-training on drifting jailbreaks can cut a classifier's false-negative rate from 4% down to 0.3% — a real, measured result — and a "rapid response" paper (arXiv 2411.07494, RapidResponseBench) argues for defending by proliferating observed jailbreaks rather than waiting for the next full retrain. On the product side, 0DIN's "SusFactor" classifier is trained on a continuously-growing, bug-bounty-sourced feed with periodic retraining. None of it is built for hours-scale viral spread specifically, and none of it is production-proven at that cadence — the academic work is academic-only, the vendor claims are self-reported. The general "keep a classifier fresh against drift" problem has active research behind it; the specific "keep pace with a technique that goes viral on Reddit in an afternoon" problem does not.

## The Project

Fresh Fence is a feed-to-retraining pipeline, not a static model. It watches the surfaces where jailbreaks actually surface first — security-research feeds, disclosed red-team writeups, and an opt-in community-submission channel modeled on how 0DIN sources its own bug-bounty feed — for techniques that are newly circulating. Every new submission gets clustered and checked for novelty against what the classifier's current training set already covers, so the pipeline isn't retraining on noise or near-duplicates of things it already catches. Genuinely novel clusters trigger a scheduled retraining or few-shot-augmentation pass, run daily rather than quarterly — the JailbreaksOverTime result at a cadence actually matched to how fast these things spread, instead of the cadence most teams happen to have inherited. Nothing ships automatically: every retrained model has to clear a held-out evaluation gate before it's promoted, specifically so a bad or poisoned retraining pass can't quietly make production worse while trying to make it better.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: teams operating production guardrail classifiers that rely on a static or infrequently-retrained model (the same `NeMo Guardrails`/`Guardrails AI`/`LLM Guard` user base, combined ~18k GitHub stars, as a proxy for deployment scale) whose coverage decays specifically against fast-circulating, publicly known jailbreak techniques rather than slow organic drift.
**Mechanism**: a classifier trained on a snapshot stays blind to a jailbreak technique for as long as the gap between disclosure and the next retraining cycle — often weeks to months — even though the JailbreaksOverTime result shows weekly retraining closes most of that gap. A feed tuned to hours-to-days cadence, with automated clustering and a held-out eval gate, closes the window specifically for techniques that are already circulating publicly, rather than waiting for the next scheduled model refresh.
**Measure**: time from a jailbreak technique first appearing in the monitored feed to the classifier being retrained and passing the held-out eval gate against it (target: single-digit days, down from a team's current retraining cadence); false-negative rate on a held-out set of newly disclosed techniques over time, replicating the JailbreaksOverTime 4%→0.3% result at a tighter cadence; false-positive rate added to legitimate traffic after each retraining pass (must stay flat or improve, not regress).

## Try It

There's already a Claude Code skill built for the sharper, smaller problem this project would eventually make unnecessary to ask manually: [`skills/viral-jailbreak-continuous-retraining-loop/`](../skills/viral-jailbreak-continuous-retraining-loop/). Its one-line description: "Point-in-time exposure audit for a production jailbreak/guardrail classifier — given the classifier's training cutoff and coverage, and a handful of newly disclosed (often hours-old, viral) jailbreak examples, estimates whether each would likely bypass the live classifier and recommends a retraining urgency tier and cadence."

In other words, before you build the always-on feed-and-retrain infrastructure described above, this skill gives you a same-day answer to "would this specific new jailbreak get past what we're running right now, and how urgent is it?" To use it: paste the full README.md into any AI chat tool (Claude, ChatGPT, etc.) along with your classifier's training coverage and the new examples you're worried about — or, if you're on Claude Code, drop the `skills/viral-jailbreak-continuous-retraining-loop/` folder into `~/.claude/skills/` and it'll be available automatically. It's explicitly a one-shot diagnostic, not a running monitor — it won't retrain anything or touch your live classifier.

Worth noting up front: the skill's own guardrails section is honest that this is a reasoning-based mechanism-overlap estimate against whatever training coverage you describe, not a measured test run against your actual production classifier — treat a "likely bypasses" verdict as something to go verify, not as confirmed fact.
