# Parity Gate: A Language-Quality-Parity Safety Layer for Community-Health-Worker Chatbots

An LLM chatbot answering real clinical questions can outperform a human clinician and cost a fraction as much per response — until you ask it the question in the language the frontline health worker actually speaks. Then, quietly, it gets a little worse. No warning, no flag, nothing to tell the person relying on the answer that the version they got was weaker than the one the model could have given them in English.

## The Gap

This isn't a hunch — it's a measured finding. A Rwanda community-health-worker (CHW) study put LLMs up against 5,609 real clinical questions asked by 101 CHWs across four districts and found the models beat clinicians on quality and cost — but "performance degraded slightly when LLMs communicated in Kinyarwanda" versus English, on the exact same questions. It isn't a one-off either: a Malawi Chichewa chatbot evaluation and the Sakhi/SimPPL WhatsApp maternal-health chatbot (English, Hindi, Marathi) each independently flag the same local-language quality gap. The projects explicitly trying to fix it — the George Institute's SMARThealth Pregnancy chatbot, IDRC's Beshte project in Kenya — are both still in co-design stages. The research has done the hard part of proving the gap is real and large enough to matter. Nobody has yet built the thing that acts on that finding the moment it happens, in a live deployment, instead of in a research paper months later.

## The Project

The fix isn't retraining the model or waiting for a better multilingual release — it's a middleware safety layer that sits between the chatbot's LLM backend and whatever channel it talks over (WhatsApp, Telegram, SMS). For every question that comes in, it silently generates two answers: the local-language one the CHW actually sees, and a parallel English answer to the same question. Then it scores how well they agree — not on tone, but on the things that matter clinically: are the same key facts present, do dosage or referral-threshold numbers match, does the hedging and urgency language line up. When the local-language answer scores below a calibrated agreement threshold, it doesn't just go out anyway. The system either falls back to a verified back-translation of the English answer, or routes the response to a human clinical supervisor for review, with the specific discrepancy called out. Over time, the logs reveal where degradation clusters — by topic, by phrasing, by language — turning "we think the local-language answers are sometimes worse" into a map of exactly where and why.

Four pieces make this real: a dual-generation step that produces both answers per question; an agreement scorer built on key-fact extraction and comparison, not just embedding similarity; a fallback/flagging policy with a tunable threshold; and a clustering log that aggregates discrepancies for program staff to act on structurally, not one incident at a time.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: Frontline community health workers who rely on LLM-chatbot clinical guidance in a local/regional language rather than English or French — WHO/secondary estimates put the global CHW workforce above 1.3 million, heavily concentrated in exactly the low- and middle-income countries where the local-language chatbots in the digest (Kinyarwanda, Chichewa, Hindi/Marathi) operate, and where a wrong dosage or missed referral threshold has direct patient-safety consequences.
**Mechanism**: Today a degraded local-language answer reaches the CHW with no signal that it's worse than the same model's English answer would have been — the CHW has no way to know to double-check. A parity check turns an invisible quality gap into a visible, actionable flag, routing only the risky minority of answers to a safer fallback or human review instead of trusting every local-language response equally.
**Measure**: (1) rate of flagged-vs-unflagged answers that a clinical reviewer independently judges unsafe or incomplete (precision/recall of the flag against a human-labeled gold set, replicating the Rwanda study's 5,609-question evaluation design); (2) reduction in the measured quality gap between local-language and English answers on matched question pairs, before vs. after fallback routing; (3) CHW-reported trust/confidence in chatbot answers in the local language, pre/post deployment.

## Try It

A companion Claude Code skill, [`skills/chw-chatbot-language-quality-parity-gate/`](../skills/chw-chatbot-language-quality-parity-gate/) — the "CHW Chatbot Language-Quality Parity Gate" — is already written: *"Audits exported community-health-worker chatbot Q&A logs for clinically meaningful gaps between a local-language answer and the same chatbot's English answer to the same question, and ranks the riskiest discrepancies for a clinical supervisor to review."*

This is a retrospective batch audit, not the live middleware itself — a clinical supervisor or M&E staffer exports a batch of past conversations (local-language question/answer plus the same question re-asked in English) and gets back a ranked report of CRITICAL/MAJOR/MINOR discrepancies with plain-language explanations. It's the cheap way to validate the scoring logic, or to run a periodic quality check, before anyone commits to wiring the always-on version into a live deployment. To use it: paste the `README.md` into any AI chat tool, or drop the `skills/chw-chatbot-language-quality-parity-gate/` folder into `~/.claude/skills/` if you're on Claude Code.

## Risks & Open Questions

Running two generations per question roughly doubles inference cost and adds latency — it needs to stay cheap enough (a smaller, faster model for the English shadow-answer, say) that it doesn't erase the "far cheaper per response" economics the Rwanda study credited LLMs with in the first place. A fallback that routes too many answers to human review defeats the purpose of automating CHW support at all — threshold calibration is the actual product here, and it needs real clinical input, not a default guess. There's also a real chance this is already being solved quietly inside SMARThealth Pregnancy or Beshte's ongoing co-design work, which is worth checking directly before building rather than assuming the gap is still open. And back-translation-as-fallback can introduce its own errors — it needs its own accuracy check, not an assumed-safe default.
