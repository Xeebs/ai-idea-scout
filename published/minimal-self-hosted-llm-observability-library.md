Every few weeks someone ships the same blog post: "I built my own LLM observability tool, here's why." They're not showing off — they're explaining why they couldn't just adopt one of the platforms everyone already knows about. Multiply that post by a year and a half of recurrence, add a wave of 2026 acquisitions swallowing the tools people were already leaning on, and you get a quiet, persistent signal: small teams building LLM products don't have a default they trust, so they keep rebuilding the same thin slice of infrastructure from scratch.

## The Gap

The pattern isn't a single loud complaint — it's the same complaint surfacing independently, again and again. An April 2025 r/LLMDevs thread asked "Is anyone building LLM observability from scratch?" Through 2026, multiple separate dev.to posts answered yes: "I built my own LLM observability tool, here's why and how," and more pointedly, "I got tired of LLM observability tools getting acquired so I built one that can't be." A local-first alternative called logmera picked up 37,000 downloads with zero marketing — the kind of quiet adoption that only happens when a real need goes unaddressed by the visible options. One enterprise team went as far as ripping Sentry out in favor of a purpose-built tracer rather than bend a general tool to fit.

None of these is a maintained, dominant, self-hostable default — each is a narrow, one-off answer to the same question. And the question keeps getting more urgent, not less: in 2026, acquisition churn hit the two tools a lot of small teams had settled on. Helicone slid into maintenance mode after being acquired by Mintlify; Langfuse got acquired via a ClickHouse deal. If you'd built your workflow around either, you just inherited someone else's roadmap decision. Even inside this same research digest, two well-resourced teams — PostHog hitting an eval-sampling inefficiency bug, and kodus-ai hand-rolling their own CI eval harness — were independently reinventing pieces of this exact stack rather than reaching for something off the shelf. When companies with engineering budgets keep rebuilding instead of buying, that's not noise, that's a market telling you something about fit, not awareness.

## The Project

The idea is Lean Log: a deliberately minimal, dependency-light, self-hostable LLM observability library — not a lite version of an enterprise platform, but a genuinely different product with a scope contract it refuses to break. It wraps your existing OpenAI/Anthropic/etc. SDK calls in-process, logs prompt, response, latency, cost, and errors to local SQLite (Postgres optional if you need it), and serves that through a lightweight local web UI. No SaaS dependency, no telemetry phoning home, packaged as a single importable module. The design goal baked into the name — "un-acquirable" — is structural, not aspirational: because it's forkable and has nothing holding your data hostage, it keeps working the same way whether or not anyone ever maintains the upstream repo again.

What differentiates it from the dev.to posts and from logmera isn't the plumbing — wrapping an SDK call and writing to SQLite has been trivial for years — it's the discipline. A published, enforced scope boundary that explicitly refuses the feature creep that pulled Langfuse and Helicone toward enterprise territory in the first place. The core components: an SDK-call wrapper capturing prompt/response/latency/cost/errors, a local storage backend (SQLite by default), a minimal browsing/filtering UI, and a documented line in the sand distinguishing it from the Langfuse/Helicone class of platform.

## Tangible Impact

**Population**: Solo developers and small (under ~10-engineer) teams running LLM-backed side projects or early-stage products who today either skip observability entirely or hand-roll a throwaway logging script, because existing platforms (Langfuse, Helicone, LangSmith, Arize) are scoped for teams with dedicated ops/eval engineers and carry SaaS lock-in or acquisition risk. The recurring "I built my own because nothing fit" pattern across multiple independent 2025–2026 posts, plus the real 2026 Helicone/Langfuse acquisition churn cited in this digest, is the direct evidence this population exists and is currently unserved by a maintained default.

**Mechanism**: Gives this population a maintained, zero-dependency, self-hosted option instead of a one-off script they alone maintain or a platform sized for a team they don't have — reducing how often a small team flies blind on LLM cost, latency, and failure rate because the "proper" tools felt like overkill to adopt.

**Measure**: (1) time from install to viewing a first logged trace, target under 5 minutes; (2) 30-day retention — share of pilot users still using it rather than having abandoned back to no observability; (3) resource footprint (memory, deploy steps) measured against the named heavier platforms on the same workload.

To use it: paste the contents of its `README.md` into any AI chat tool (ChatGPT, Claude, etc.) along with your stack, request volume, team size, any data-residency constraint, and your current pain point — it'll tell you which of the four tiers fits before you spend engineering time on the wrong one. If you're on Claude Code, drop the whole `skills/minimal-self-hosted-llm-observability-library/` folder into `~/.claude/skills/` and it activates automatically when you ask about observability tooling decisions.

Two honest caveats worth carrying forward from the idea file rather than glossing over: "minimal" is a moving target, and the same scope-creep pressure that pulled Langfuse and Helicone toward enterprise features will pull on this project too — the scope contract has to be enforced in practice, not just stated at launch. And logmera, with 37k quiet downloads, is already close to this exact positioning; without concrete differentiation (the published scope contract, the explicit non-acquisition structure), this risks becoming a fourth narrow attempt rather than the dominant one. There's also a measurement irony built into the design: a library with zero telemetry by principle is, by the same principle, hard to measure adoption for from the outside, so its own success metrics depend on opt-in pilot relationships rather than passive analytics.
